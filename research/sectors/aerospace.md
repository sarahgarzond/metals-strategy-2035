# Aerospace

Notes only for section 5.4. The section file was not edited. "The company" is the client. Haynes International and VDM Metals are one Acerinox group and are counted once. VDM's portfolio is an application benchmark only, not evidence of what the company can make or whom it already supplies. No alloy mass per engine is estimated.

Scope split used throughout: commercial aircraft (airframes and airline fleets), engines (civil and military gas turbines, including maintenance), and defence (platforms and budgets). Engine metal demand sits with the engine makers and their casters and forgers, not with Airbus or Boeing as buyers of nickel alloy.

## Key findings

1. Airbus GMF 2026 (July 2026 passenger deck, aircraft of at least 100 seats) puts the in-service passenger fleet at 23,310 at the end of 2025 and 45,550 at the end of 2045. Meeting that path takes 42,060 new passenger aircraft in 2026 to 2045: 33,920 typically single aisle and 8,140 typically widebody, of which 22,240 are for growth, 19,820 for replacement, and 3,490 of today's aircraft stay. Passenger traffic grows 3.9% a year from 9.9 trillion RPK in 2025 to 21.3 trillion in 2045. Source: data/raw/aerospace/airbus_gmf_2026_presentation.pdf, slides 12 and 33 to 38. [AERO_AIRBUS_GMF2026_FLEET_2025, AERO_AIRBUS_GMF2026_DELIVERIES, AERO_AIRBUS_GMF2026_RPK]
2. Boeing CMO 2026, on a wider commercial fleet that includes regional jets and freighters, puts the fleet at about 28,000 airplanes in 2025, about 38,000 in 2035 and about 50,000 in 2045. Deliveries in 2026 to 2045 are 43,625: 33,545 single aisle, 7,715 widebody, 1,435 regional jets and 930 production freighters. Jet fleet growth is 3.0% a year and passenger traffic 4.0% a year. About half of deliveries replace older airplanes (21,475 replacement and 22,150 growth). Source: Boeing presentation, pages 18 to 20 and 24, and the 18 July 2026 press release. [BOEING_CMO_2026, AERO_BOEING_CMO2026_FLEET_2035, AERO_BOEING_CMO2026_FLEET_CAGR]
3. Airbus does not publish a 2035 passenger fleet count. A constant growth path between 23,310 and 45,550 implies about 32,600 aircraft in 2035 (team estimate, formula below). That figure must not be cited as an Airbus number. The published 2035 milestones are different objects: Airbus says new-generation aircraft are 39% of the western passenger fleet at the end of 2025 and 82% in 2035, and Boeing says 32%, 75% and 92% new generation in 2025, 2035 and 2045 on its wider fleet. [AERO_AIRBUS_GMF2026_NEWGEN_2035]
4. The measurable cobalt indicator, not a nickel tonne figure, is what exists. The Cobalt Institute, using Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, reports 11,499 tonnes of cobalt in aviation in 2025, with commercial applications at 72% and military jets at 2.6 thousand tonnes. Superalloys as a whole took 20 thousand tonnes, up 7.5% on 2024. Alloy-grade metal from the four named refinery sources was only about 15 thousand tonnes. USGS says an estimated 51% of US cobalt consumption in 2025 went to superalloys, mainly aircraft gas turbines, on estimated US consumption of 8,000 tonnes. No official nickel use in aerospace was found. [AERO_CI_AVIATION_CO_2025, AERO_USGS_CO_SUPER_SHARE]
5. Engine programmes, not airframe assembly, are the company's market. CFM delivered 1,802 LEAP engines in 2025, up 28% from 1,407 in 2024. GE Aerospace's commercial engine deliveries were 2,386 in 2025, and that total already includes the 1,802 LEAP engines, so the two figures must not be added. GE defence engine deliveries were 635, against 490 in 2024. Rolls-Royce still describes parts availability as constrained. [AERO_LEAP_2025, AERO_GE_COMM_ENG_2025]
6. Services are the profit pool and a large share of metal pull. Airbus GSF 2026 puts the services market at USD 209 billion in 2026 and USD 422 billion in 2045, a 3.8% a year path, for passenger aircraft above 100 seats and freighters above 10 tonnes. Off-wing maintenance grows at 3.7% a year and Airbus says over 85% of it is driven by materials. GE's Commercial Engines and Services segment took 75% of its 2025 revenue from services. [AERO_AIRBUS_GSF2026_SERVICES_2045]
7. Defence budgets are not a defence-aerospace metal market. SIPRI's April 2026 fact sheet puts world military expenditure at USD 2,887 billion in 2025, up 2.9% in real terms, with Europe up 14% to USD 864 billion and US spending down 7.5% to USD 954 billion. Company filings show defence engine and platform work is real but smaller than civil engines: MTU military revenue was EUR 614 million in 2025, against commercial MRO of EUR 5,960 million. [AERO_SIPRI_MILEX_2025, AERO_MTU_MIL_2025]
8. Qualified melt, forge and casting capacity is still in the United States, the United Kingdom, France and Germany, while airline deliveries shift toward Asia. Airbus assigns about 19,190 of 42,060 passenger deliveries in 2026 to 2045 to China, India and the rest of Asia-Pacific. The buyers of nickel bar, billet and master alloy are GE Aerospace, Safran, Rolls-Royce, Pratt and Whitney, MTU, Howmet, ATI and PCC, not the Asian airlines.
9. The open risk is substitution and captive conversion, not a lack of engine programmes. Titanium aluminide already flies in the Pratt geared-turbofan low-pressure turbine. Durability kits aim to cut shop visits per engine. GE has agreed to buy a castings supplier. Safran is doubling captive forging at Gennevilliers. Airframers are closing the titanium scrap loop. None of these removes the nickel hot section in the years to 2035, and none has a published kilogram displacement.

### Commercial aircraft

Team estimate for an Airbus-perimeter 2035 stock: 23,310 multiplied by the square root of 45,550 / 23,310, which is 32,585, reported as about 32,600. The calculation assumes a constant compound path and ignores delivery timing. It is not used as a market size below. The published Boeing 2035 stock of about 38,000 is the figure to cite when a 2035 fleet is required, with the wider definition stated in the same sentence.

Near-term deliveries are still the constraint. Airbus delivered 793 commercial aircraft in 2025 (A220 93, A320 family 607, A330 36, A350 57) and guides about 870 in 2026, with the A320 family aimed at 70 to 75 a month by the end of 2027. Boeing delivered 600 in 2025 (737 447, 767 30, 777 35, 787 88), against 348 in 2024. Combined 2025 deliveries were 1,393. Backlogs cover many years: Airbus 8,754 commercial aircraft and EUR 619 billion at the end of 2025, Boeing Commercial Airplanes about USD 612 billion and more than 6,300 aircraft. IATA measured 2025 passenger traffic at +5.3% in RPK, capacity at +5.2% in ASK and the load factor at a full-year record of 83.6%. International RPK was +7.1% and domestic +2.4%. Airbus's own July 2026 deck then shows 2026 traffic at +2.1% versus full-year 2025, so the long-term 3.9% is not the next-year rate. ICAO's long-term route-group table, base year 2018, has world RPK growth of 4.2% a year over 10, 20 and 30 years. That table is older than GMF and CMO 2026 and is used only as a cross-check.

Services, which pull spare parts, are published. Airbus GSF 2026: USD 209 billion in 2026 and USD 422 billion in 2045, +3.8% a year. The draft section's "over USD 420 billion" matches the 2045 level. The draft's blank for "today" does not: 2026 is USD 209 billion. The draft's off-wing figure of USD 217 billion was not in the local GSF text. What the deck does say is that off-wing maintenance grows at 3.7% a year and that over 85% of it is driven by materials, on a scope of passenger aircraft above 100 seats and freighters above 10 tonnes.

A dedicated freighter line on the Airbus GMF web page (not repeated in the local passenger slide deck) says the dedicated freighter fleet expands 46% by 2045 and that 2,670 new freighters are required. Treat that as a webpage figure pending a cargo-deck page check. Boeing's 930 is production freighters only, and Boeing separately says more than 2,900 production and converted freighters. Those two freighter totals are not the same object.

The previous logged Airbus outlook, AIRBUS_GMF_2025, is 43,420 deliveries over 2025 to 2044. It is a prior edition and a different window. It should not be averaged with 42,060.

High-performance metal demand inside the airframe is not given a dollar size here. Boeing's 10-K names aluminium, titanium and composites as the raw materials it buys. Nickel reaches the airframe mainly inside the engine, the pylon and fasteners. No airframe nickel market value is published.

### Engines

There is no official world engine-delivery total and no official nickel tonne total. The published programme facts are the market.

Civil. Safran and GE both report 1,802 LEAP deliveries in 2025 because CFM is their joint venture. Safran's first half of 2026 was 1,030 LEAP engines, up 41%, and GE's first nine months of 2026 were 1,565 LEAP engines, up 26%, and 1,989 commercial engines, up 25%. The draft sentence that GE engine deliveries "rose 25%" belongs to the first nine months of 2026, not to full-year 2025. Full-year 2025 commercial deliveries at GE were 2,386, so LEAP was about three quarters of that commercial total. GE's installed base is about 50,000 commercial engines and about 30,000 military engines, and services are about 70% of GE Aerospace revenue. Commercial Engines and Services revenue was USD 33,314 million in 2025 with a 26.6% margin. Remaining performance obligation was USD 190.6 billion, of which USD 163.0 billion is services.

Rolls-Royce civil aerospace revenue was about GBP 10.4 billion in 2025, about half of group underlying revenue, at a 20.5% margin. Large-engine flying hours grew 8%. Guidance for 2026 is 550 to 600 new original-equipment deliveries and 1,480 to 1,550 shop visits, with flying hours at 115% to 120% of 2019. Safran expects 2,300 to 2,400 CFM56 shop visits a year from 2025 to 2028.

MTU, a risk-and-revenue partner rather than a prime engine OEM, reported adjusted revenue of EUR 8,700 million in 2025: OEM EUR 2,870 million, commercial MRO EUR 5,960 million, military EUR 614 million. Commercial MRO covers more than 1,300 shop visits a year, and the geared turbofan was about 40% of commercial MRO revenue. The medium-term target is EUR 13 to 14 billion of revenue in 2030.

Pratt and Whitney (RTX) sales were USD 32,916 million in 2025. The organic increase included USD 2.9 billion of commercial aftermarket and USD 0.9 billion of commercial OEM. PW1100G-JM shop-visit output rose about 26% in 2025. The powder-metal inspection keeps extra shop visits in the plan through the end of 2026.

Growth to 2035 for engine metal is not a published tonne forecast. The direction from the filings is that original-equipment deliveries stay supply-limited into the late 2020s, while shop visits rise on the CFM56, the geared turbofan and the Trent fleet, and that a larger LEAP and Trent installed base carries the 2030s. Durability improvements work the other way and are recorded under risks. A cobalt path that simply grows the 11,499 tonnes at 3.4% a year, which is how the draft reaches about 16,100 tonnes in 2035, is not used. Superalloy cobalt grew 7.5% in a single year, military jet cobalt is mostly maintenance, and engine mix is changing.

## 2. Growth drivers and risks

### Commercial aircraft

Drivers. Airbus ties the 3.9% traffic path to GDP growth of 2.6% a year from 2025 to 2045, a larger middle and upper class, and more direct routes between smaller cities. City pairs rose from 17,800 in 2005 to 28,000 in 2025, and 78% of the increase involved small cities. The single-aisle share of Airbus deliveries is 81% (33,920 of 42,060), which concentrates volume on LEAP and the geared turbofan. Replacement is the second driver: only 3,490 of the end-2025 passenger fleet is still in service in 2045 on the Airbus path, and new-generation types rise from 39% to 82% by 2035. Boeing's story is the same shape: passenger traffic 4.0%, replacement about half of deliveries, low-cost carrier fleets growing faster than network carrier fleets (3.9% against 2.6% in the CMO text).

IATA's 2025 commentary is the near-term version of the same point. Demand grew faster than seats, airlines kept older aircraft flying, and the supply chain cost was described as above USD 11 billion. Load factors cannot absorb much more growth. That supports deliveries, and it also means metal demand arrives late and unevenly when engines are short.

Risks. Airbus's July 2026 deck already marks 2026 traffic at +2.1%, not +5.3%. The Middle East disruption is drawn as a shock that does not change the 20-year path, which is an assumption, not a measured result. Boeing still lost USD 7.1 billion in Commercial Airplanes in 2025, including reach-forward losses on the 777X, so the widebody ramp that pulls the largest engines is not on the original schedule. First 777-9 delivery is planned for 2027. Qualification of a new metal source takes a year or more even where Boeing says alternative sources "generally exist". Rate increases (A320 at 75 a month, 737 toward 47) fail when engines, wings or interiors fail, which delays the alloy call-off rather than cancelling the backlog.

### Engines

Drivers. A larger installed base, a maintenance bulge on CFM56 and on geared-turbofan inspections, and new programmes (GE9X, Trent XWB, LEAP, GTF Advantage) all at once. GE has had ten consecutive quarters of sequential growth in priority supplier material. Safran's chief executive said in April 2026 that 2026 LEAP deliveries would exceed 2,000 and that demand should keep exceeding supply. Rolls-Royce is adding shop-visit capacity (target another 20% by the medium term, including Beijing up to 250 Trent overhauls a year by the mid-2030s and Istanbul up to 200 shop visits a year by the end of 2027). Howmet's Engine Products sales were USD 4,320 million in 2025 at a 33.3% adjusted EBITDA margin, and third-quarter 2026 engine-product sales were up 33%, citing commercial aerospace, gas turbines and spares.

Risks. Parts are still short: Rolls-Royce guides a GBP 150 to 200 million cash impact from the supply chain in 2026, "similar to 2025, with parts availability improving but still constrained". Airbus has said Pratt and Whitney engine shortages weigh on deliveries. The powder-metal issue on the geared turbofan is a quality failure in a rotating part, with a 2026 cash impact of about USD 0.7 billion at RTX and incremental shop visits through 2026. That raises near-term spare-part demand and raises the cost of a bad powder qualification for anyone who wants to supply discs. Durability is the structural risk to repair volume: the LEAP-1B high-pressure-turbine blade kit is expected to roughly double time on wing, and Rolls-Royce targets more than a 100% durability improvement on in-production Trent engines by the end of 2027, with more than half of that already delivered in 2025. Captive conversion is the other risk. Safran is adding a 30,000 tonne press at Gennevilliers (EUR 150 million, about 14,000 parts a year at full rate from 2029, site output nearly doubled by 2035) for LEAP, GE9X and military forgings in nickel superalloy and titanium. GE has signed to buy Consolidated Precision Products for USD 11.75 billion, closing expected in the second half of 2027. A secondary report says that business is about a quarter of GE's casting needs and that GE keeps ATI and Howmet as suppliers. The purchase price is a filing fact. The quarter share is not.

Input risk sits on cobalt more than on nickel price. World Bank Pink Sheet nickel averaged USD 15,162 a tonne in 2025, down from USD 16,814 in 2024 (arithmetic mean of the twelve months in data/clean/worldbank_pinksheet_monthly.csv). December 2025 was USD 14,884 a tonne. The clean file has no cobalt series and stops at December 2025. USGS puts mined cobalt at 310,000 tonnes in 2025, with Congo (Kinshasa) at 230,000 tonnes (73%) and Indonesia at 44,000 tonnes. Export quotas replaced the 2025 ban: 18,125 tonnes contained for the rest of 2025, and up to 96,600 tonnes a year in 2026 and 2027, including 9,600 tonnes for a strategic reserve. The Cobalt Institute's mined figure is 270 thousand tonnes and total supply 295 thousand tonnes. The gap versus USGS is about 40 thousand tonnes and is not resolved here. USGS is used for the country split. The Institute is used for end use. Alloy-grade rounds are a narrower bottleneck than mine output: about 15 thousand tonnes from Glencore Nikkelverk, Sumitomo Metal Mining Niihama, Vale Port Colborne and Long Harbour, and Jinchuan.

### Defence

Drivers. European rearmament shows up in SIPRI and in order books (RTX defence backlog, Airbus Defence and Space order intake, F135 production and sustainment). Military engines are less cyclical than airline OEM. GE defence deliveries rose from 490 to 635 in one year. Risks. The world spending increase slowed to 2.9% because the largest spender cut. MTU's military revenue was flat on supply chain, so budget growth does not equal parts growth. Export licences, offsets and domestic-content rules, especially in the United States and China, limit who can sell rotating-part alloy. Western Superconducting is the named Chinese producer for domestic aero-engine bar, and its audited annual report was not opened, so its 2025 revenue (secondary summaries: RMB 5.226 billion, high-end titanium 53%) is not a firm market size.

## 3. Emerging technologies and applications

### Commercial aircraft

The technology that changes metal demand before 2035 is the new-generation airframe already in production, not a new alloy family. Airbus defines new generation as the A220, A320neo family, A330neo, A350, 737 MAX, 787 and 777X. Those types are 39% of the western passenger fleet now and 82% in 2035. They use more composites and titanium in the airframe than the types they replace, and they use new engines. The A321neo is 75% of the A320-family backlog in the Airbus deck, so the volume engine is a single-aisle engine, not a widebody core. Range extensions (A321XLR routes of 2,500 to 4,700 nautical miles in the deck) let single-aisle aircraft take missions that used to need a small widebody. That shifts some metal from two large engines toward two smaller ones. The kilogram effect is not published and is not estimated.

Airbus is industrialising titanium revert. Since 2024, EcoTitanium has recycled titanium process scrap at Saint-Eloi in Toulouse, extended in 2025 to Varel and Augsburg. In June 2025 EcoTitanium and IMET Alloys produced a first ingot from end-of-life titanium pylon material qualified for aerospace use. That reduces primary sponge demand for airframe parts. It does not supply engine rotating nickel alloy.

Boeing's raw-material sentence is unchanged in substance: aluminium and titanium in sheet, plate, forgings and extrusions, plus composites. Structural titanium share by programme, including the often quoted A350 figure, was not tied to a page in the Airbus profile and is left as a gap.

### Defence

Named engine families in the filings and profiles, without any mass: GE F110, F404, F414 and T408, Pratt F135 and F119, Safran M88 (Rafale) and TP400 (A400M). The Cobalt Institute defence table also lists Ni/Co superalloy in aircraft combustors and in integrated electric propulsion for aircraft carriers, and samarium-cobalt magnets in sensors, missile actuators and naval motors. Drone batteries are a cobalt use (2.6 thousand tonnes in defence batteries for drones in the same report) and are not a superalloy market. Fighter and transport engines stay on nickel hot sections. No new defence alloy system with a published displacement of nickel was found.

USGS titanium, 2026 summaries: the United States produced no titanium sponge in 2025. The last domestic sponge plant closed in 2024. Apparent sponge imports were an estimated 44,000 tonnes, and the customs value of imported sponge was about USD 460 million. The majority of US titanium metal went to aerospace, with the rest in armour, chemical processing, marine hardware, medical implants, power generation and other uses. No aerospace share in percent, and no aerospace tonne figure, is published. Through July 2025 the leading sponge import sources were Japan (73%), Kazakhstan (13%) and Saudi Arabia (13%). That is a partial-year trade split.

USGS cobalt: estimated US consumption 8,000 tonnes in 2025, 51% superalloys mainly for aircraft gas turbines, 25% chemical, 15% other metallic, 9% cemented carbides. The 51% and the 8,000 tonnes are both published. A product of the two is not published and is not stated here as a USGS figure.

Cobalt Institute aviation: 11,499 tonnes in 2025. Commercial 72% (about 8,300 tonnes if multiplied, a reading of the chart, not a second published total). Military jets 2.6 thousand tonnes. The chart also shows slices of 23%, 3% and 2% beside the 72% commercial share. 2.6 / 11.499 is 23%, so the 23% slice matches military jets. Which of the 3% and 2% is business aviation and which is civil rotorcraft is not labelled in the text and is not assigned. Superalloys in all uses, 20 thousand tonnes, are wider than aviation. Defence applications across superalloys, batteries and magnets, 6.8 thousand tonnes.

ATI's own nickel use was about 70 million pounds in 2025. That is one competitor's melt, not the industry, and a one-dollar move in the nickel price is about USD 70 million of ATI cost, mostly passed through. Carpenter sold 207.9 million pounds of all products, not of aerospace alloy alone.

Grades. Do not treat a catalogue as a demand forecast. The only grade names carried forward, and only as provisional, are CMSX-4 and RR1000 on Trent blades, because the Rolls-Royce profile states them and the underlying standard was not opened. Inconel and Incoloy are Special Metals trademarks for a wide nickel range, not a single aerospace specification. Haynes 282 and the C-276 family are Haynes products now inside Acerinox, relevant as the competitive set for heat-resistant sheet and bar. TiAl on the PW1100G-JM is a named competing material, not a nickel grade.

## 5. Geographic concentration of demand

Airline demand and mill demand are different maps.

## 8. Where high performance metals gain or lose relevance

Gains.

* Every new single-aisle delivery to 2035 still needs a nickel hot section. Single-aisle aircraft are 81% of Airbus deliveries and about 77% of Boeing deliveries (33,545 of 43,625). The new-generation fleet goes from a minority to a large majority by 2035, and those engines are not a return to older alloys.
* Shop visits add a second demand wave. Off-wing maintenance is the services line Airbus ties most directly to materials (+3.7% a year, over 85% driven by materials). Military jet cobalt is already 86% maintenance. CFM56 shop visits of 2,300 to 2,400 a year through 2028 are a dated, finite wave. LEAP and Trent visits build behind them.
* Spare-part and powder-metal replacements on the geared turbofan support disc alloy through 2026 and show how little qualified capacity exists when a rotor-material issue appears.
* Defence engines and European spending support a baseload, especially F135 sustainment and European engine modules, without being the volume case.
* Industrial gas turbines are a spillover, not an aerospace end use. Howmet cites data-centre electricity demand. Haynes' backlog commentary inside Acerinox cites the same. It uses the same nickel grades and the same mills. It is recorded so it is not mistaken for aircraft demand.

Losses and caps.

* Titanium aluminide has already replaced nickel in low-pressure turbine blades of the PW1100G-JM. Further TiAl or ceramic matrix composite use in the hottest static parts would cap nickel in those components. The displacement in tonnes is not published.
* Durability kits reduce shop visits per engine. Fleet growth and the young LEAP fleet can outweigh that for a time. The sign of the net effect after 2030 is not measured.
* Composites and titanium take structural share on new widebodies, and aluminium remains the large airframe metal on single-aisle aircraft. That is a loss for anyone whose aerospace story is airframe nickel. It is not a loss for engine nickel. Boeing lists composites beside aluminium and titanium, and says alternative sources generally exist for those airframe metals, with a qualification time of a year or more.
* Closed-loop titanium (EcoTitanium, and MTU returning titanium chips) caps primary sponge growth for the airframe and the fan. It does not cap nickel master alloy.
* Captive forging and captive casting move the profit pool from independent forgers toward the OEM. The mill can still sell billet and master alloy into a captive shop. It cannot assume the forging conversion stays open.
* Chinese domestic qualification walls off the fastest-growing airline market from European and US rotating-part exports. Western Superconducting is the evidence that the wall is being filled from inside China.

## 9. Attractive parts of the value chain for the company

Judged on published margins, lead times, who holds the specification, and whether the step stays open to an outside mill. The company is treated as a high-performance alloy producer. Nothing below assumes a current approval, a current share, or a VDM reference list.

Most attractive: vacuum-melted nickel and nickel-cobalt bar, billet and master alloy for engine hot sections, sold to the engine OEMs and to the casters and forgers they already qualify (Howmet, ATI, PCC, Safran Gennevilliers). Evidence that the step earns its keep: Carpenter specialty alloy operating margin 27.7%, ATI forgings and components adjusted EBITDA margin 23.6%, Howmet Engine Products adjusted EBITDA margin 33.3%, against ATI's standard alloy products at 16.3%. Evidence that capacity is the constraint: GE's multi-year supply constraint, ATI isothermal forging lead times of more than two years and premium titanium of about 20 months, Rolls-Royce parts still constrained in 2026. The buyer's engineer sits in the United States, France, the United Kingdom and Germany. Starting with Safran is a geographic convenience for a European mill. It is not a substitute for GE, Rolls-Royce and Pratt qualifications, because those three plus Safran are the specification holders ATI already serves.

Attractive, and slower to qualify: welding wire and repair alloy for off-wing maintenance. The services market doubles on Airbus's numbers by 2045, off-wing work is materials-heavy, and military as well as civil cobalt demand is mostly repair. Special Metals already lists welding products, so the shelf is occupied. The company's opening is a second qualified source while shops are short of parts, not a commodity wire price.

Selective: powder for discs and for additive parts. The geared-turbofan powder issue proves the value of a qualified powder source and the cost of a defect. Carpenter's powder and additive segment made a 7.9% operating margin while its premium bar business made 27.7%. Powder is not where the profit is today. Enter it only against a named disc or additive specification.

Less attractive as a growth bet: standard airframe aluminium, and primary titanium sponge and standard titanium sheet. Airframers dual-source those products more readily, composites take structure on widebodies, and Airbus is designing scrap back into aerospace ingot. Titanium forgings for fans, pylons and landing gear remain a real adjacent market, with Japanese sponge as the supply risk, and they are not the nickel franchise.

Defence is a qualification to hold, not the volume plan. MTU's military revenue is about one tenth of its commercial MRO. SIPRI's USD 2,887 billion is not an addressable market. European budget growth and F135 sustainment justify the cost of defence approvals because they smooth civil cycles. They do not replace LEAP and Trent demand.

What the company should do, as research conclusions rather than section text:

* Put qualification effort on nickel superalloy bar, billet and master alloy for LEAP, GTF, Trent and GE9X hot sections, with Safran, GE, Rolls-Royce, Pratt, MTU and Howmet as the named doors. Approval times are measured in years in every filing that discusses a new source.
* Treat ATI, Carpenter, Special Metals and the Acerinox Haynes and VDM business as one competitive set. Do not plan as if Haynes and VDM were two separate mills to displace.
* Build a repair-alloy and welding offer against the shop-visit bulge (CFM56 through 2028, geared-turbofan inspections, Trent shop visits of 1,480 to 1,550 in Rolls-Royce's 2026 guide), and watch durability kits so the offer is not sized on a shop-visit rate that the OEMs are trying to cut.
* Keep powder as a watched option tied to a disc specification. Do not lead with additive.
* Buy or contract alloy-grade cobalt with more than one of the named refinery sources. Mine diversification away from Congo does not by itself produce aerospace-grade metal. Nickel is the easier input on the evidence of the Pink Sheet and is mostly passed through by the mills.
* Do not spend the commercial effort on Asian airlines. Spend it where the engines are forged and overhauled. Revisit China only as a separate, likely closed, chain.
