# 3. Introduction to the Analytical Framework and Methodology

The analysis follows a metal value chain that runs from material through product form, application, industry and geography to market size and growth, and Table 1 shows how the chapters cover its links. A figure is only compared with a figure from the same stage, so contained metal is kept apart from gross weight and output from capacity. The evidence starts with structured statistics from the USGS, UN Comtrade, Eurostat PRODCOM, the World Bank, OECD, UNIDO and the IEA, continues with industry associations, official outlooks and the filings of 61 companies, and uses commercial market reports only as a cross check. Every key figure is confirmed by a second independent source, and the research was organised in specialised workstreams for each metal, each industry and the company universe, whose findings were consolidated into a shared workbook in which every value carries its unit, stage, data type, sources and formula.

Table 1: The metal value chain and its evidence

| Link | Guiding question | Main evidence | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material | Where is the metal mined and refined, and how concentrated is supply? | USGS, IEA critical minerals data, World Bank prices | 4 |
| Product form | In which qualified forms does the metal reach industry? | Eurostat PRODCOM, producer filings | 4 |
| Application | Which components need the material, and why? | Standards, associations, filings | 4 and 5 |
| Industry and geography | Which industries and regions buy, and how fast do they grow? | Airbus, Boeing, IEA, OICA, Cefic, SEMI, OECD | 5 |
| Market size | How large is measurable demand today? | Apparent consumption, trade values, end use statistics | 5 and 6 |
| Growth | How large could demand be in 2035, and what could add to it? | Calibrated demand model and emerging opportunities register | 7 |

*Source: team framework.*

The outlook to 2035 is built in two tiers that are never mixed. The first tier is a demand model for established applications, which multiplies the activity of each industry, such as engine deliveries, gigawatts of gas turbines or joint replacements, by the metal content per unit and by the share of units that use a high performance alloy. This model is calibrated to reproduce published history, back tested by fitting it on data up to 2019 and comparing its projection for 2020 to 2025 with actual outcomes, and corrected for the past optimism of official outlooks, while a Monte Carlo simulation yields the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of 2035 demand and a supply overlay tests them against export quota and licence shocks.

The second tier covers opportunities that the data cannot yet confirm, such as hydrogen fuelled turbines, supercritical carbon dioxide recuperators, molten salt and small modular reactors, fusion magnet substrate tape or additively manufactured engine parts. Each candidate is drawn from patents, pilot plants, research programmes, standards under development or announced projects and assessed on its technology readiness, the strength of its evidence, the alloys and product forms it would need and the date by which it could reach commercial volume. These options are reported as a separate register with qualitative ranges and signposts, such as a final investment decision or a first serial order, that would move them into the model, and chapter 8 scores both tiers on the same eight criteria while flagging the evidence behind each score, so that measured positions are never confused with options that should be monitored rather than funded at scale.
