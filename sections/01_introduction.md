# 1. Introduction

For centuries, metals have been among the materials on which human societies are built, from cities and transport networks to power grids, machinery and the technologies of modern economies. Their uses have widened with each wave of industrial development, and the sector remains large: according to McKinsey & Company's Global Materials Perspective, the metals and mining industry generated about USD 3.0 trillion in revenue in 2024 after a decline of 6 percent and grew by only about 1 percent in 2025 [MCK_GMP_2025, MCK_GMP_2026].

Within this broad market, high performance alloys serve the conditions that ordinary materials cannot withstand, such as the hot section of a jet engine, a sour gas well, a chemical reactor or a permanent implant. These engineered materials combine nickel, cobalt, chromium, molybdenum, titanium, tungsten and other elements to provide strength at high temperature, corrosion and oxidation resistance or biocompatibility, and they reach industry as qualified bar, billet, sheet, strip, wire, tube and powder. Their raw materials, however, come from a small number of countries, as Indonesia mines about 63 percent of the world's nickel, the Democratic Republic of the Congo about 73 percent of its cobalt and China most of its tungsten [IEA_CM_NI_SUPPLY_2025_2035, CO_USGS_MINE_DRC_2025, USGS_MCS2026_DATA].

The outlook for these alloys to 2035 is positive but uneven, and its size is still poorly measured. Commercial estimates place the segment between about USD 10 billion and USD 14 billion in 2025, a spread that reflects different market boundaries rather than different markets [IMARC_HPA_2025, TIP_HPA_2025]. Part of the future demand is already visible in engine and turbine programmes, energy investment and the supply of alloying metals that export policy increasingly shapes, while another part depends on technologies such as hydrogen turbines, advanced reactors and additive manufacturing that official statistics do not yet capture. This study therefore measures the first part from official production, trade and industry data and treats the second as a set of emerging opportunities whose evidence and timing are assessed openly rather than hidden in a single headline figure.
