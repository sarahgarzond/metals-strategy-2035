# 2. Report Objective and Scope

This project studies selected metals that are essential to high performance alloys and follows them from the mine to the industrial applications that depend on them, in order to assess how their markets may develop through 2035. It asks one central question: how do the concentration of metal supply, the conversion of metals into qualified product forms and the needs of seven industries determine where the most attractive opportunities in high performance materials will lie through 2035? Four subsidiary questions structure the answer, and each maps onto one part of the report. The first asks where the ten metals are mined and refined, how exposed that supply is to concentration and export policy, and in which qualified forms the metals reach industry. The second asks which industries and regions drive demand for these forms today and how large the measurable market is. The third asks how large demand could become by 2035, how certain that estimate is and which emerging applications could add to it, and the fourth asks where supply security, growth and entry barriers combine into the most attractive opportunities.

The scope covers ten metals, namely nickel, cobalt, chromium, molybdenum, titanium, tungsten, zirconium, copper, aluminium and manganese, and seven industries, namely automotive, oil and gas, energy and environmental technology, aerospace, chemical processing, electronics and electrical engineering, and medical technology. The analysis is global, with regional comparisons for Europe, North America and Asia, and it uses the most recent official data available in November 2026. Mine output, refined metal, alloy melt, mill products and industry revenue measure different stages of the value chain, so the report never adds them into one aggregate market value. The outlook has two parts, a quantified estimate of demand for established applications and a structured assessment of emerging applications that may matter by 2035 but are not yet visible in official statistics.

The report argues that value in this market is created by converting metals into qualified products rather than by mining them. Nickel is in surplus, while cobalt, tungsten and molybdenum are constrained by export policy, and the countries that convert metals into qualified bar, strip and tube earn trade surpluses that the mining countries do not. Chapter 3 explains how this argument is tested, chapters 4 to 7 follow the metals from supply through industry demand and trade to growth, chapter 8 identifies where the most attractive opportunities lie and chapter 9 concludes.
