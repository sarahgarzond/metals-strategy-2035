# 2. Report Objective and Scope

This project studies selected metals that are essential to high performance alloys and follows them from the mine to the industrial applications that depend on them, in order to assess how their markets may develop through 2035. It asks one central question: how do the concentration of metal supply, the conversion of metals into qualified product forms and the needs of seven industries determine where the most attractive opportunities in high performance materials will lie through 2035? Four subsidiary questions structure the answer, and each maps onto one part of the report. The first asks where the ten metals are mined and refined, how exposed that supply is to concentration and export policy, and in which qualified forms the metals reach industry. The second asks which industries and regions drive demand for these forms today and how large the measurable market is. The third asks how large demand could become by 2035, how certain that estimate is and which emerging applications could add to it, and the fourth asks where supply security, growth and entry barriers combine into the most attractive opportunities.

The study covers ten metals: nickel, cobalt, chromium, molybdenum, titanium, tungsten, zirconium, copper, aluminium, and manganese. It examines seven industries: automotive, oil and gas, energy and environmental technology, aerospace, chemical processing, electronics and electrical engineering, and medical technology. The study uses the latest official data available in November 2026. It covers the global market and compares Europe, North America, and Asia. The outlook quantifies established uses and evaluates emerging uses separately because official statistics do not yet capture them.

The report argues that much of the value in this market comes from turning metals into qualified products. Nickel is in surplus, while export policies restrict supplies of cobalt, tungsten, and molybdenum. Countries that make qualified bar, strip, and tube earn trade surpluses on those products that mining countries do not.

Measured in tonnes, the largest growing pool is superalloy bar, billet and remelt stock for engines and turbines. It rises from about 193 kt in 2025 to 248 kt in 2035, worth EUR 6.3 to 11.7 bn [MODEL_FAMILY_VALUE_2026]. By index, electronics grows fastest at 141, ahead of aerospace and medical technology at 136, energy at 132 and automotive at 102 [MODEL_T1_RESULTS_2035]. Electronics tracks fab capacity with unmeasured alloy volume, so it yields a priority 2 position in fab gas strip and tube. Priority 1 goes to turbine remelt stock and sheet and to Alloy 718 engine bar, both at 3.85, for buyers in the United States and Europe. As one vacuum melt programme, they reach priority 1 under three of four weightings [MODEL_COMBINED_PROGRAMME_2026].

The ranking is conditional, since a tighter Congo cobalt quota or a severe aerospace downturn each moves one of them to priority 2 [MODEL_SUPPLY_SHOCKS_2026]. Priority 2 covers engine bar in all grades, CoCr implant bar, Alloy 690 and additive powder, while FeNi36 mask foil and NO96 strip remain options. Producers should stop investing in diesel only parts, new European base chemical plants, unfunded electrolyser capacity and captive zirconium cladding.
