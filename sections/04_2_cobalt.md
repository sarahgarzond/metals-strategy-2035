## 4.2 Cobalt

Cobalt is the scarcest of the major superalloy elements, and its scarcity is created by policy rather than by geology. USGS estimates world mine output at 310 kt of contained cobalt in 2025, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo produced about 230 kt or 73 percent, while China refined 78.6 percent of the world's 240 kt of refined cobalt [CO_USGS_MINE_WORLD_2025, CO_USGS_MINE_DRC_2025, CO_CI_REFINED_2025, CO_CI_CHINA_REF_2025]. In February 2025 Congo banned exports and then imposed a quota of up to 96,600 t a year for 2026 and 2027, CMOC, which mined 117,549 t in 2025, received an export entitlement of only 31,200 t, and the European cobalt metal price rose 130 percent during 2025 [CO_USGS_QUOTA_2026, CO_CMOC_CO_2025, CO_CMOC_QUOTA_2026, CO_CI_METAL_PRICE_2025]. The IEA expects mine supply to fall to 225 kt by 2035 against demand of 300 to 322 kt, so the shortage is structural [IEA_CM_CO_SUPPLY_2025_2035, IEA_CM_CO_DEMAND_2025_2035].

The alloy industry is a small customer in this market, but it buys the most demanding product form. Batteries for vehicles and portable electronics took 206 kt of cobalt in 2025 against only 20 kt for superalloys, which buy mostly high purity metal from a few Western refineries [CO_CI_EV_2025, CO_CI_PORTABLE_2025, CO_CI_SUPER_2025]. Cobalt reaches its applications as superalloy remelt stock for turbine blades and combustors, as cobalt chromium bar and powder for hip and knee implants, and as wear resistant alloys. Because these applications cannot easily switch to another metal, a quota decided in Kinshasa becomes a cost and continuity risk in Toulouse and Cincinnati, and chromium shows a similar dependence one step later in the chain.
