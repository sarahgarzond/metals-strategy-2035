## 4.2 Cobalt

Cobalt is the scarcest of the major superalloy elements, and its scarcity is created by policy rather than by geology. World mine production reached an estimated 310 kt of contained cobalt in 2025, of which the Democratic Republic of the Congo produced about 230 kt, or 74 percent of the rounded total, while China refined 78.6 percent of the world's 240 kt of refined cobalt [CO_USGS_MINE_WORLD_2025, CO_USGS_MINE_DRC_2025, CO_CI_REFINED_2025, CO_CI_CHINA_REF_2025]. In February 2025 Congo banned exports and then replaced the ban with a quota of up to 96,600 t a year for 2026 and 2027, and CMOC, which mined 117,549 t in 2025, received an export entitlement of only 31,200 t [CO_USGS_QUOTA_2026, CO_CMOC_CO_2025, CO_CMOC_QUOTA_2026]. The European cobalt metal price reacted by rising 130 percent during 2025 [CO_CI_METAL_PRICE_2025].

The alloy industry is a small customer in this market, but it buys the most demanding product form. Batteries for electric vehicles and portable electronics took 206 kt of cobalt in 2025, while superalloys took only 20 kt, mostly as high purity metal from a few Western refineries [CO_CI_EV_2025, CO_CI_PORTABLE_2025, CO_CI_SUPER_2025]. Cobalt reaches its applications as superalloy remelt stock for turbine blades and combustors, as cobalt chromium bar and powder for hip and knee implants, and as wear resistant alloys. Because these applications cannot easily switch to another metal, a quota decided in Kinshasa becomes a cost and continuity risk in Toulouse and Cincinnati, and the next metal, chromium, shows a similar dependence one step later in the chain.
