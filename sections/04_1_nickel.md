## 4.1 Nickel

Nickel is the foundation of the high performance alloy industry, and within a decade its supply has moved to a single country. World mine production of contained nickel reached an estimated 3.90 Mt in 2025, of which Indonesia supplied about 2.60 Mt, roughly two thirds of the total [NI_USGS_MINE_W_2025, NI_USGS_MINE_ID_2025]. The mine is only the first stage, however, because primary nickel production reached 3.875 Mt in 2025 against usage of 3.577 Mt, and just over half of that primary supply in China and Indonesia was nickel pig iron for stainless steel rather than the class 1 metal that vacuum melted alloys require [NI_INSG_PROD_2025, NI_INSG_USE_2025, NI_INSG_NPI_2025]. Abundant nickel at the mine therefore does not guarantee abundant nickel in the grade that alloy makers buy.

This oversupply explains the price, which averaged USD 25,834 per tonne in 2022 and USD 15,162 in 2025 [NI_WB_AVG_2022, NI_WB_AVG_2025]. Indonesia has responded by managing output through its annual ore quota, cutting the 2026 quota to 260 to 270 Mt of ore before raising it to 290 to 300 Mt, and the International Nickel Study Group consequently cut its 2026 surplus forecast from 299 kt in 2025 to only 19 kt [ARGUS_NI_RKAB_2026, NI_RKAB_2026_REV, NI_INSG_BAL_2025, NI_INSG_BAL_2026]. The price of the raw material is now set as much in Jakarta as on the London Metal Exchange.

Nickel reaches industry in two fundamentally different product forms, and the gap between them reveals where value is created. Stainless steel absorbs most nickel, with world melt shop output of 64.2 Mt in 2025, while nickel alloys are sold as strip, sheet, plate, bar, wire and tube for chemical plants, oil and gas wells, gas turbines and jet engines [NI_WS_MELT_2025]. German exports of nickel plate, sheet and strip reached 17,775 t worth USD 578 million in 2024, an average of about USD 32,500 per tonne or roughly twice the exchange price of the contained metal [NI_CT_DE_7506_2024_T]. Buyers clearly pay for qualification and form rather than for nickel, a pattern that becomes sharper still for cobalt, whose supply is constrained by policy rather than geology.
