## 4.4 Molybdenum

Molybdenum is what turns ordinary stainless steel into the alloys that survive hot acids and seawater, and its intensity varies enormously between grades. One tonne of 316L stainless contains about 21 kg of molybdenum, whereas one tonne of Alloy C-276 contains about 160 kg and Alloy 625 about 90 kg, so a small volume of nickel alloy carries a large molybdenum bill [MO_OUTOKUMPU_316L_MO, MO_HAYNES_C276_MO, MO_ATI_625_MO].

Supply is tied to copper mining, which limits its response to price, and USGS estimates world mine output at 260 kt of contained molybdenum in 2025, while the International Molybdenum Association reports 672.6 million pounds, about 305 kt in a team conversion, and almost the entire gap sits in China [MO_USGS_MINE_WORLD_2025, MO_IMOA_PROD_2025, MO_USGS_MINE_CHINA_2025]. According to USGS only China and the United States produce from primary molybdenum mines, and since February 2025 China has licensed exports of molybdenum powders rather than of the oxide and ferromolybdenum that melt shops buy [MO_USGS_MINE_US_2025, W_MOFCOM_2025_10]. The U.S. oxide price rose 7 percent to an estimated USD 51 per kg in 2025, while European use fell 7 percent with the weak chemical cycle [MO_USGS_PRICE_OXIDE_2025, MO_IMOA_USE_EUROPE_2025]. Molybdenum thus links corrosion alloys to copper mining and Chinese policy, whereas titanium competes with nickel alloys more than it feeds them.
