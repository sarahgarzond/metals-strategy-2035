## 4.4 Molybdenum

Molybdenum is what turns ordinary stainless steel into the alloys that survive hot acids and seawater, and its intensity varies enormously between grades. One tonne of 316L stainless contains about 21 kg of molybdenum, whereas one tonne of Alloy C-276 contains about 160 kg and Alloy 625 about 90 kg, so a small volume of nickel alloy carries a large molybdenum bill [MO_HAYNES_C276_MO, MO_ATI_625_MO]. This intensity makes molybdenum one of the most important cost drivers of the corrosion resistant alloys used in chemical processing and oil and gas.

Supply is moderately concentrated and is tied to copper mining in a way that limits its response to price. USGS estimates world mine output at 260 kt of contained molybdenum in 2025, while the International Molybdenum Association reports about 305 kt, and almost the entire gap sits in China [MO_USGS_MINE_WORLD_2025, MO_IMOA_PROD_2025, MO_USGS_MINE_CHINA_2025]. Only China and the United States operate primary molybdenum mines, so most supply follows the copper mine plan, and China has added molybdenum products to its export licence regime since February 2025 [W_MOFCOM_2025_10]. The U.S. oxide price rose 7 percent to an estimated USD 51 per kg in 2025, while European use fell 7 percent with the weak chemical cycle [MO_USGS_PRICE_OXIDE_2025, MO_IMOA_USE_EUROPE_2025]. Molybdenum thus links the economics of corrosion alloys to copper mining and Chinese policy, whereas titanium, the next metal, competes with nickel alloys more than it feeds them.
