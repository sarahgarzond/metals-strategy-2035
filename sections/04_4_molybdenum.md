## 4.4 Molybdenum

Molybdenum separates ordinary stainless steel from the corrosion alloys that earn the highest premiums, and its intensity differs by two orders of magnitude. One tonne of 316L contains about 21 kg of molybdenum, whereas Alloy C-276 contains about 16 percent and Alloy 625 about 9 percent [MO_HAYNES_C276_MO, MO_ATI_625_MO]. A small volume of nickel alloy therefore carries a large molybdenum bill.

Molybdenum supply is moderately concentrated, and a large part of it is a byproduct of copper mining. USGS estimates world mine output at 260 kt of contained molybdenum in 2025, while the International Molybdenum Association reports about 305 kt, a gap that sits almost entirely in China [MO_USGS_MINE_WORLD_2025, MO_IMOA_PROD_2025, MO_USGS_MINE_CHINA_2025]. Only China and the United States mine molybdenum both as a primary product and as a byproduct, so most supply follows the copper mine plan rather than the molybdenum price. The U.S. oxide price rose 7 percent to an estimated USD 51 per kg in 2025 [MO_USGS_PRICE_OXIDE_2025].

Europe remains the second largest user, although its use fell 7 percent in 2025, which reflects the weak chemical cycle described in chapter 5 [MO_IMOA_USE_EUROPE_2025]. For the company molybdenum is a pass through cost under surcharges, and its price links the profitability of the C-276 and Alloy 59 product lines to copper mining. Titanium, the next metal, is less an input than a competitor and partner material.
