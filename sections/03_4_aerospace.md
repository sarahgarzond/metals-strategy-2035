## 3.4 Aerospace

<!-- sector-analyst aerospace -->

Aerospace and defence is the sector where the Client's nickel and cobalt alloys earn the highest value per kilogram, and our market model puts the sector's demand for the Client's addressable materials at EUR 7.5bn in 2025, rising to EUR 14.8bn by 2035 at 7.0% p.a. (team estimate) [MODEL_AERO_2025]. This scope excludes titanium and covers nickel and cobalt alloys, specialty stainless steels, aluminium and copper products, powders and filler metals sold into civil aviation, defence and space, which is about 15% of the EUR 50.6bn addressable market [MODEL_TOTAL_2025]. The estimate looks conservative, because ATI and Carpenter alone sell roughly USD 5bn a year into aerospace and defence (team estimate from reported sales and segment shares) [ATI_FY2025, CRS_FY2026], which is why the growth evidence matters more than the base figure.

### Market size, drivers and risks

Growth through 2035 rests on a civil backlog that already fills the decade, since Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025, holds a record backlog of 8,754 aircraft and targets about 870 deliveries in 2026 [AIRBUS_DELIV_2025]. Boeing recovered to 600 deliveries in 2025 [BOEING_DELIV_2025], and both OEMs expect roughly 43,500 new aircraft over twenty years, about three quarters of them single aisle jets [AIRBUS_GMF_2025, BOEING_CMO_2026]. Engines translate this backlog into metal demand, as CFM delivered 1,802 LEAP engines in 2025, up 28%, while GE's priority suppliers shipped more than 40% more material than a year earlier [GE_LEAP_2025]. The aftermarket adds a second engine, because Howmet grew engine product sales by 16% on the back of rising spares demand [HWM_ENGINE_2025], although the wider maintenance market grows by only 3.3% p.a. to 2036 [OW_MRO_2026], so the near term boom will settle towards fleet growth after about 2030 (team assessment).

Defence and space add a structural layer that civil cycles cannot erase, because world military spending reached USD 2,887bn in 2025 with European spending up 14% [SIPRI_MILEX_2025], and NATO members committed to 3.5% of GDP for core defence by 2035 [NATO_HAGUE_2025]. The space economy is projected to grow from USD 630bn in 2023 to USD 1.8 trn by 2035 [WEF_SPACE_2035], although most of that value is services, so the hardware share relevant to metals is far smaller. SpaceX flew 165 Falcon 9 missions in 2025 [SPACEX_LAUNCH_2025], which shows that launch cadence now supports serial production of engine parts rather than single prototypes. The main risks are engine supply bottlenecks, which already cap the A320 ramp at 70 to 75 aircraft a month by end 2027 [AIRBUS_DELIV_2025], quality escapes, tariffs and qualification cycles that keep new suppliers waiting for years, and these risks shape which technologies the Client should back.

### Emerging technologies and materials required

Additive manufacturing and powder metallurgy are the technologies that move furthest into the Client's portfolio, because GE's Auburn plant had shipped 100,000 printed LEAP fuel nozzle tips by 2021, each replacing about 20 brazed parts and weighing about 25% less [GE_AM_NOZZLE]. GE patents name cobalt chromium powder for such nozzles, but the LEAP alloy is not confirmed in our sources, so TODO(data): confirm the nozzle alloy. Space propulsion creates a copper niche, since the NASA copper chromium niobium alloy GRCop-42 is now sold as gas atomised powder by KBM Advanced Materials and Sandvik Osprey for regeneratively cooled chambers [GRCOP_KBM_2023]. ### Geography, customers and competitors

Demand for semifinished metal concentrates where engines and forgings are made, which means the United States, France, the United Kingdom and Germany, even though Asia-Pacific will take 46% of new passenger aircraft to 2044 [AIRBUS_GSF_APAC]. Engine makers GE Aerospace, Safran, Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce and MTU Aero Engines and their forgers Howmet, PCC Wyman Gordon, Aubert & Duval, Otto Fuchs and Leistritz specify the alloys, while Airbus, Boeing, COMAC and Embraer buy aluminium and stainless content mostly through Tier 1 suppliers. Supply is concentrated in a few mills, as Table 3.4b shows, and incumbents are adding melt capacity, with ATI building a fifth vacuum induction furnace of 9 kt a year in Monroe for late 2027 [ARGUS_SUPERALLOY] and Carpenter adding about 9,000 tons in Athens, Alabama, for early fiscal 2028 [CRS_ATHENS]. OEMs also integrate backwards, as Airbus, Safran and Tikehau Ace agreed in 2022 to buy Aubert & Duval at an enterprise value of EUR 95m [AD_ACQ_2022], which signals how much value buyers place on secure superalloy supply.

### Where high performance metals gain or lose relevance

Substitution runs in both directions, because ceramic matrix composites at about one third of nickel alloy density and up to 2,400 °F [GE_CMC_PROPS] now replace nickel in LEAP shrouds and GE9X liners and nozzles [ACERS_CMC_LEAP], while rotating discs and shafts remain nickel alloy territory. In airframes, the Boeing 787 uses 50% composites, 15% titanium and only 20% aluminium by weight [B787_MATERIALS], which shifts value away from aluminium and towards titanium and composites in widebody aircraft. Nickel and cobalt alloys gain in hotter and more numerous engines, in repair, in printed parts and in rocket engines, which is where the Client can position its existing forms.

### Attractive value chain steps for the Client

The most attractive entry points sit downstream of the large billet melters, because thin strip, sheet and wire in 718, 625, 282 and X750 match the Client's forms and face fewer competitors than rotating grade billet (team assessment). Welding wire and repair powders follow the spares boom, and nickel, cobalt and copper powders grow fastest at about 15% p.a. in our model cell, while defence and space sheet and bar grow at about 9% p.a. (team estimates) [MODEL_AERO_2025]. Large rotating grade billet and aluminium plate are less attractive for the Client, given incumbent capacity additions and scale, and these priorities feed directly into the opportunity matrix in chapter 4.
