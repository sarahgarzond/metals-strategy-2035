1.     Introduction

For centuries, metals have been among the materials on which human societies are built, from
cities and transport networks to power grids, machinery and the technologies of modern
economies. Their uses have widened with each wave of industrial development, and the sector
remains large: according to McKinsey & Company's Global Materials Perspective, the metals
and mining industry generated about USD 3.0 trillion in revenue in 2024 after a decline of 6
percent and grew by only about 1 percent in 2025 (McKinsey & Company, 2025, 2026).

Within this broad market, high performance alloys serve the conditions that ordinary materials
cannot withstand, such as the hot section of a jet engine, a sour gas well, a chemical reactor or
a permanent implant. These engineered materials combine nickel, cobalt, chromium,
molybdenum, titanium, tungsten and other elements to provide strength at high temperature,
corrosion and oxidation resistance or biocompatibility, and they reach industry as qualified
bar, billet, sheet, strip, wire, tube and powder. Their raw materials, however, come from a
small number of countries, as Indonesia mines about 63 percent of the world's nickel, the
Democratic Republic of the Congo about 73 percent of its cobalt and China most of its
tungsten (International Energy Agency, 2026b; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026a, 2026e).

The outlook for these alloys to 2035 is positive but uneven, and its size is still poorly
measured. Commercial estimates place the segment between about USD 10 billion and USD
14 billion in 2025, a spread that reflects different market boundaries rather than different
markets (IMARC Group, 2025; The Insight Partners, 2025). Part of the future demand is
already visible in engine and turbine programmes, energy investment and the supply of
alloying metals that export policy increasingly shapes, while another part depends on
technologies such as hydrogen turbines, advanced reactors and additive manufacturing that
official statistics do not yet capture. This study therefore measures the first part from official
production, trade and industry data and treats the second as a set of emerging opportunities
whose evidence and timing are assessed openly rather than hidden in a single headline figure.


2.     Report Objective and Scope

This project studies selected metals that are essential to high performance alloys and follows
them from the mine to the industrial applications that depend on them, in order to assess how
their markets may develop through 2035. It asks one central question: how do the
concentration of metal supply, the conversion of metals into qualified product forms and the
needs of seven industries determine where the most attractive opportunities in high
performance materials will lie through 2035? Four subsidiary questions structure the answer,
and each maps onto one part of the report. The first asks where the ten metals are mined and
refined, how exposed that supply is to concentration and export policy, and in which qualified
                                                                                             4
forms the metals reach industry. The second asks which industries and regions drive demand
for these forms today and how large the measurable market is. The third asks how large
demand could become by 2035, how certain that estimate is and which emerging applications
could add to it, and the fourth asks where supply security, growth and entry barriers combine
into the most attractive opportunities.

The scope covers ten metals, namely nickel, cobalt, chromium, molybdenum, titanium,
tungsten, zirconium, copper, aluminium and manganese, and seven industries, namely
automotive, oil and gas, energy and environmental technology, aerospace, chemical
processing, electronics and electrical engineering, and medical technology. The analysis is
global, with regional comparisons for Europe, North America and Asia, and it uses the most
recent official data available in November 2026. Mine output, refined metal, alloy melt, mill
products and industry revenue measure different stages of the value chain, so the report never
adds them into one aggregate market value. The outlook has two parts, a quantified estimate of
demand for established applications and a structured assessment of emerging applications that
may matter by 2035 but are not yet visible in official statistics.

The report argues that value in this market is created by converting metals into qualified
products rather than by mining them. Nickel is in surplus, while cobalt, tungsten and
molybdenum are constrained by export policy, and the countries that convert metals into
qualified bar, strip and tube earn trade surpluses that the mining countries do not. Chapter 3
explains how this argument is tested, chapters 4 to 7 follow the metals from supply through
industry demand and trade to growth, chapter 8 identifies where the most attractive
opportunities lie and chapter 9 concludes.


3.     Introduction to the Analytical Framework and Methodology

The analysis follows a metal value chain that runs from material through product form,
application, industry and geography to market size and growth, and Table 1 shows how the
chapters cover its links. A figure is only compared with a figure from the same stage, so
contained metal is kept apart from gross weight and output from capacity. The evidence starts
with structured statistics from the USGS, UN Comtrade, Eurostat PRODCOM, the World
Bank, OECD, UNIDO and the IEA, continues with industry associations, official outlooks and
the filings of 61 companies, and uses commercial market reports only as a cross check. Every
key figure is confirmed by a second independent source, and the research was organised in
specialised workstreams for each metal, each industry and the company universe, whose
findings were consolidated into a shared workbook in which every value carries its unit, stage,
data type, sources and formula.

The outlook to 2035 is built in two tiers that are never mixed. The first tier is a demand model
for established applications, which multiplies the activity of each industry, such as engine
deliveries, gigawatts of gas turbines or joint replacements, by the metal content per unit and by
the share of units that use a high performance alloy. This model is calibrated to reproduce
published history, back tested by fitting it on data up to 2019 and comparing its projection for
2020 to 2025 with actual outcomes, and corrected for the past optimism of official outlooks,
while a Monte Carlo simulation yields the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of 2035 demand and
a supply overlay tests them against export quota and licence shocks.

The second tier covers opportunities that the data cannot yet confirm, such as hydrogen fuelled
turbines, supercritical carbon dioxide recuperators, molten salt and small modular reactors,
fusion magnet substrate tape or additively manufactured engine parts. Each candidate is drawn
from patents, pilot plants, research programmes, standards under development or announced
projects and assessed on its technology readiness, the strength of its evidence, the alloys and
product forms it would need and the date by which it could reach commercial volume. These
options are reported as a separate register with qualitative ranges and signposts, such as a final
investment decision or a first serial order, that would move them into the model, and chapter 8
scores both tiers on the same eight criteria while flagging the evidence behind each score, so
that measured positions are never confused with options that should be monitored rather than
funded at scale.

The scorecard sorts the ten metals into three groups that structure the rest of the report. Nickel
and chromium are the backbone of every alloy and are available in volume, cobalt, tungsten,
molybdenum and aerospace grade titanium are scarce because of policy or qualification, and
copper, aluminium and manganese matter mainly as complementary or competing materials,
so nickel, the base of the whole alloy family, comes first.


4.1    Nickel

Nickel is the foundation of the high performance alloy industry, and within a decade its supply
has moved to a single country. USGS estimates world mine output at 3.90 Mt of contained
nickel in 2025, with 2.60 Mt or about two thirds from Indonesia, while the IEA puts the total
at 4.21 Mt and Indonesia at 63 percent of mining and 45 percent of refining, so the series
differ in level but not in concentration (International Energy Agency, 2026b; U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026h). Primary production reached 3.875 Mt against usage of 3.577 Mt, yet just over
half of the primary supply in China and Indonesia was nickel pig iron for stainless steel rather
than the class 1 metal that vacuum melted alloys require (International Nickel Study Group,
2025, 2026). Alloy makers therefore depend on a few class 1 sources such as Vale in Canada,
Glencore Nikkelverk in Norway and Nornickel in Russia, and Russian metal produced on or
after 13 April 2024 may no longer enter the United States or be warranted on metal exchanges
under US and UK sanctions (U.S. Department of the Treasury, 2024; United Nations Statistics
Division, 2026a).

This oversupply explains the price, which averaged USD 25,834 per tonne in 2022 and USD
15,162 in 2025 in the World Bank series, against a USGS estimate of USD 15,000 (U.S.
Geological Survey, 2026h; World Bank, 2026a). Indonesia now manages output through its
annual ore quota, cutting the 2026 quota to 260 to 270 Mt of ore before raising it to 290 to 300
Mt, and the International Nickel Study Group cut its 2026 surplus forecast to 19 kt after 299 kt
in 2025, so the price of the raw material is now set in Jakarta as much as in London (Argus
Media, 2026a; Indonesia Business Post, 2026; International Nickel Study Group, 2026).

Nickel reaches industry in two fundamentally different product forms, and the gap between
them reveals where value is created. Stainless steel absorbs most nickel, with 64.2 Mt of gross
melt shop output in 2025, and the IEA expects nickel demand outside energy technologies,
which covers stainless steel and alloys, to grow only from 2,906 kt in 2025 to 3,255 kt in 2035
(International Energy Agency, 2026b; worldstainless, 2026). Nickel alloys are sold as strip,
sheet, plate, bar, wire and tube, and German exports of nickel plate, sheet and strip reached
17,775 t worth USD 578 million in 2024, about USD 32,500 per tonne or twice the price of
the contained metal, a tonnage that Eurostat confirms within 2 percent (Eurostat, 2026a;
United Nations Statistics Division, 2026f; World Bank, 2026a). Buyers pay for qualification
and form rather than for nickel, a pattern that cobalt makes sharper still.


4.2    Cobalt

Cobalt is the scarcest of the major superalloy elements, and its scarcity is created by policy
rather than by geology. USGS estimates world mine output at 310 kt of contained cobalt in
2025, close to the IEA figure of 320 kt, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo produced
about 230 kt or 73 percent, while China refined 78.6 percent of the world's 240 kt of refined
cobalt (Cobalt Institute, 2026; International Energy Agency, 2026b; U.S. Geological Survey,
2026e). In February 2025 Congo banned exports and then imposed a quota of up to 96,600 t a
year for 2026 and 2027, CMOC, which mined 117,549 t in 2025, received an export
entitlement of only 31,200 t, and the European cobalt metal price rose 130 percent during
2025 (CMOC Group, 2026a, 2026b; Cobalt Institute, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026e).
The IEA expects mine supply to fall to 225 kt by 2035 against demand of 300 to 322 kt, so the
shortage is structural (International Energy Agency, 2026b).

The alloy industry is a small customer in this market, but it buys the most demanding product
form. Batteries for vehicles and portable electronics took 206 kt of cobalt in 2025 against only
20 kt for superalloys, which buy mostly high purity metal from a few Western refineries
(Cobalt Institute, 2026). Cobalt reaches its applications as superalloy remelt stock for turbine
blades and combustors, as cobalt chromium bar and powder for hip and knee implants, and as
wear resistant alloys. Because these applications cannot easily switch to another metal, a quota
decided in Kinshasa becomes a cost and continuity risk in Toulouse and Cincinnati, and
chromium shows a similar dependence one step later in the chain.

4.3    Chromium

Chromium gives every corrosion and heat resistant alloy its protective oxide layer, yet its
value chain is split between the countries that mine it and the country that smelts it. World
chromite ore output reached an estimated 51 Mt gross weight in 2025, of which South Africa
mined about 23 Mt on USGS figures and 25.2 Mt on the differently defined dashboard of the
International Chromium Development Association (International Chromium Development
Association, n.d.; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026d). The same association reports 9.9 Mt of
ferrochrome alloy for China, a smelter product that cannot be set against ore tonnes, from a
country with almost no chromite (International Chromium Development Association, n.d.).
Stainless steel absorbs most of this ferrochrome, and the United States imports 79 percent of
its chromium needs (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026d; worldstainless, 2026).

The product form that high performance alloys require is far more specialised than the
ferrochrome that stainless mills consume. Vacuum melted nickel alloys use low carbon
ferrochrome and aluminothermic chromium metal, a small market whose U.S. price rose to an
estimated USD 5.90 per pound in 2025 while the ferrochrome price fell (U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026d). A single qualified source of this small input can therefore halt a vacuum melt
shop, a vulnerability that molybdenum repeats in an even more value intensive form.


4.4    Molybdenum

Molybdenum is what turns ordinary stainless steel into the alloys that survive hot acids and
seawater, and its intensity varies enormously between grades. One tonne of 316L stainless
contains about 21 kg of molybdenum, whereas one tonne of Alloy C-276 contains about 160
kg and Alloy 625 about 90 kg, so a small volume of nickel alloy carries a large molybdenum
bill (ATI, n.d.; Haynes International, 2023; Outokumpu, 2025).

Supply is tied to copper mining, which limits its response to price, and USGS estimates world
mine output at 260 kt of contained molybdenum in 2025, while the International Molybdenum
Association reports 672.6 million pounds, about 305 kt in a team conversion, and almost the
entire gap sits in China (International Molybdenum Association, 2026; U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026g). According to USGS only China and the United States produce from primary
molybdenum mines, so most other supply follows the copper mine plan, and since February
2025 China has licensed exports of molybdenum powders rather than of the oxide and
ferromolybdenum that melt shops buy (Ministry of Commerce of the People's Republic of
China, 2025a; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026g). The U.S. oxide price rose 7 percent to an
estimated USD 51 per kg in 2025, while European use fell 7 percent with the weak chemical
cycle (International Molybdenum Association, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026g).
Molybdenum thus links corrosion alloys to copper mining and Chinese policy, whereas
titanium competes with nickel alloys more than it feeds them.

4.5    Titanium

Titanium replaces nickel alloys in lighter and cooler parts while sharing their aerospace buyers
and qualification bottlenecks, and its mineral and metal markets barely overlap, since world
output of ilmenite and rutile reached 9.8 Mt of TiO2 in 2025, mostly for pigment, while
sponge metal reached only 370 kt (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026i, 2026j). China produced
about 260 kt of that sponge and holds 320 kt of the 470 kt of world capacity, but Chinese
sponge is not the qualified source for Western engines, and the United States has no operating
sponge capacity (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026i).

The scarce product form is aircraft grade sponge and the ingot, bar and plate made from it, as
Osaka Titanium is raising aircraft grade capacity from 40 kt to 50 kt by fiscal 2027, ATI quotes
lead times of about 20 months for premium titanium, and Europe melts and fabricates titanium
without producing sponge, selling 22.6 kt of wrought titanium in 2024 against imports of 31.7
kt (ATI, 2026b; Eurostat, 2024; Osaka Titanium Technologies, 2025). The same shortage of
qualified capacity constrains nickel superalloys, which strengthens every qualified melter, and
tungsten shows how quickly one government can reshape such a market.


4.6    Tungsten

Tungsten is the clearest example of a metal whose supply one state controls. World mine
output reached an estimated 85 kt of contained tungsten in 2025, of which China produced
about 67 kt, or 79 percent (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026k). China sets national mining quotas,
with a first batch of 58,000 t of concentrate for 2025, and since February 2025 it has required
export licences for ammonium paratungstate, tungsten oxides, unsintered carbide and specified
tungsten alloys, while sintered carbide tools and tungsten wire remain outside the control
(Ministry of Commerce of the People's Republic of China, 2025a, 2025b; Ministry of Natural
Resources, 2025).

Most tungsten ends up as carbide for cutting and mining tools, which takes 65 percent of first
use, while steels and superalloys take 14 percent (International Tungsten Industry Association,
n.d.). In superalloys tungsten strengthens cast turbine alloys and heat resistant sheet, so a
licence decision in Beijing soon reaches the gas turbine hot section, and Sandvik's powder
business more than doubled orders and revenues on higher tungsten prices in the third quarter
of 2026 (Sandvik, 2026). Tungsten thus closes the group of policy constrained metals before
the niche and competing metals of section 4.7.


4.7    Other Selected Metals

The remaining metals complete the picture in two ways, because zirconium and the minor
superalloy elements are niche inputs, while copper, aluminium and manganese are large
markets with a small high performance corner.
                                                                                               11
4.7.1   Zirconium

Zirconium shows why mineral output and specialty metal must be kept apart, since world
production of zirconium concentrates reached about 1.2 Mt gross weight in 2025, led by
Australia with 400 kt, mostly for ceramics, foundry sand and refractories (U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026l). The metal market is far smaller and divides into nuclear fuel cladding, which
fuel makers and integrated producers such as ATI control themselves, and zirconium plate and
tube for acid service, which is the open part of the market (ATI, 2026a).


4.7.2   Copper

Copper links the alloy market to electrification, although high performance products are a
small part of it. World mine production reached about 23.2 Mt in 2025 on ICSG and IEA
figures alike, flat rolled products take only 11 percent of first use against 63 percent for wire,
and the price set LME records above USD 14,500 per tonne in 2026 (International Copper
Study Group, 2025, 2026a; International Energy Agency, 2026b; The Oregon Group, 2026).
The IEA expects mine supply from current projects to fall to 20.7 Mt by 2035 while demand
rises from 27.8 Mt to 32.4 Mt under current policies, and it sizes the primary deficit in 2035 at
about 25 percent (International Energy Agency, 2026a, 2026b). Data centres illustrate the new
demand, since a 1 MW rack at 54 VDC needs up to 200 kg of copper busbar, and the
specialised forms are copper nickel and high conductivity strip (NVIDIA, 2025a).


4.7.3   Aluminium
Aluminium matters here mainly as a competitor, because it replaces heavier alloys wherever
temperature and corrosion allow it. World primary production reached about 74 Mt in 2025, of
which China produced 61 percent, while European primary output has shrunk to about 955 kt
against demand of about 9 Mt a year (European Aluminium, n.d.; Federation of Aluminium
Consumers in Europe, 2024; International Aluminium Institute, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey,
2026c).


4.7.4   Manganese

Manganese is an alloying addition whose tonnage follows carbon steel, and world mine output
reached about 20 Mt of contained manganese in 2025, led by South Africa, Gabon, Ghana and
Australia, which together supplied about 81 percent, and the ore price fell to about USD 4.5
per metric ton unit, so it is neither a supply risk nor a growth market for alloy producers
(International Manganese Institute, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026f).

4.7.5   Additional Metals

Iron matters through FeNi alloys such as FeNi36 for display masks and FeNi42 for lead
frames, whose value is set by rolling capability rather than by iron supply. Niobium and
rhenium matter for superalloys, because Alloy 718 contains about 5 percent niobium and
single crystal turbine blades contain rhenium, of which only about 81 t were mined worldwide
in 2025, mostly as a byproduct of copper and molybdenum (RTX, 2026; U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026b). The analysis now turns to the industries that convert these materials into
demand for alloys.


5.      Industry Needs: Demand, Geography and Market Size

Metals create value only when an industry needs them, so the chain now moves from material
to demand. Each of the seven industries follows the same links, from size and regions to the
applications and product forms through which high performance metals enter it. Because
industry revenue is not alloy demand, each section uses the most direct measurable indicator
and closes its first subsection with the best measured size at a named stage of the chain. Where
no alloy tonnage is published, the industry enters chapter 7 only as a team index, and appendix
A5 holds the indicator tables. To avoid double counting, electrolysers and fuel cells belong to
energy, process vessels to chemicals, and sensors and magnetic parts to electronics.


5.1     Automotive

Automotive is the clearest case of high performance alloys losing ground, because
electrification removes the turbochargers, exhaust sensors, glow plugs and gaskets that have
long consumed heat resistant nickel alloys.


5.1.1   Demand, Geography and Market Size

Vehicle output still grows but shifts toward China and electric cars, as the world built 96.4
million motor vehicles in 2025, 34.5 million of them in China, while EU car registrations of
10.8 million stayed 17 percent below their 2019 level (European Automobile Manufacturers'
Association, 2026a; International Organization of Motor Vehicle Manufacturers, 2026).
Electric cars exceeded 20 million sales in 2025, a quarter of the market, and the IEA expects
about half of global car sales and more than 90 percent in China and the EU to be electric by
2035 (International Energy Agency, 2026c). The battery offers little compensation, since
lithium iron phosphate cathodes, which contain no nickel, took over 55 percent of electric
vehicle batteries in 2025 (International Energy Agency, 2026c).

European policy may soften the shift, since in October 2026 the European Parliament's
industry and transport committees backed a 95 percent cut in new car CO2 emissions for 2035,
or 87.5 percent with credits for European low carbon steel, ahead of a plenary vote expected in
                                                                                                13
December 2026 (electrive, 2026). The best measured metal figure is therefore a battery figure,
IEA nickel demand from electric vehicles of 439 kt in 2025 and 1,294 kt to 1,536 kt in 2035,
which is cathode material, while engine alloy tonnage is unmeasured and declining
(International Energy Agency, 2026b).


5.1.2     Applications and Opportunities

Engine alloys decline slowly, because hybrids and gasoline engines keep turbochargers in
production for another decade. Turbocharger output reached close to 50 million units in 2025,
and Garrett expects it to fall back toward the 2022 level by 2030 while gasoline turbo
penetration peaks at 54 percent in 2032 (Garrett Motion, 2026). BorgWarner still won three
gasoline turbocharger programmes in October 2026 with production from 2027 (BorgWarner,
2026).

Implications for suppliers:

5.2       Oil and Gas

Oil and gas is a stable market for high performance metals, in which investment moves from
oil to gas and sour gas, deep water and LNG need alloys that ordinary steel cannot replace.


5.2.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size

Upstream investment holds its level, but much of it only defends existing output. World
upstream oil and gas investment fell from USD 625 billion in 2019 to USD 542 billion in 2025
                                                                                                   14
and about USD 546 billion in 2026, and about 40 percent of upstream spending only slows the
decline of existing fields (International Energy Agency, 2025b, 2026h, 2026j). Behind this flat
total, North America remains the largest region at USD 165 billion in 2026 but has fallen from
USD 219 billion in 2019, while the Middle East is the largest region spending above its 2019
level, at USD 93 billion against USD 75 billion, and its gas investment roughly doubled from
USD 22.9 billion to USD 45.4 billion (International Energy Agency, 2026j). Central and South
America grew fastest, from USD 42 billion to USD 68 billion, and Sub Saharan Africa
recovers by 12 percent in 2026, while LNG trade could grow from 560 bcm in 2024 to 880
bcm in 2035 under current policies (International Energy Agency, 2025c, 2026h, 2026j).
Upstream investment is thus the best measured stage, since no public source counts the tonnes
of nickel alloy, duplex or clad pipe that the industry buys.


5.2.2     Applications and Opportunities

The real barrier to entry in this market is customer approval rather than metallurgy. ISO 15156
defines which corrosion resistant alloys in which metallurgical condition suit sour service, so a

Standards Norway, 2011). Orders then flow through the approved vendor lists of EPC
contractors and subsea majors such as SLB and TechnipFMC, which took USD 10.1 billion of

alloy bar for wellheads and completion tools to umbilical tube, where Alleima offers hyper
duplex SAF 3007 as successor to the standard super duplex SAF 2507, and clad pipe, for
which BUTTING reports turnover of EUR 880 million (Alleima, n.d.; BUTTING, 2026).

5.3       Energy and Environmental Technology

Energy technology holds both the most urgent shortage and the most visible disappointment in
the market, because gas turbines cannot be built fast enough while hydrogen has so far
produced more announcements than orders.


5.3.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size

Gas turbines are the tightest pocket, because data centres and grid stability created more orders
than the industry can build. Siemens Energy is sold out until fiscal 2028 and will raise its gas
turbine capacity from about 17 GW in fiscal 2024 to above 30 GW a year (Siemens Energy,
2025). Investment in gas fired power almost doubled from USD 64 billion in 2019 to USD 112
billion in 2025, and data centres could use about 945 TWh of electricity in 2030, more than
twice their 2024 level (International Energy Agency, 2025a, 2026j).

Hydrogen shows the opposite picture, as low emissions production was only about 1 Mt of
more than 100 Mt of demand in 2025 and the announced 2030 pipeline shrank to 27 Mt
(International Energy Agency, 2026d, 2026e). The IEA project database counts 4.3 GW of
electrolysis in operation, 19.3 GW at final investment decision or under construction and 858
GW at concept or feasibility stage, and alkaline electrolysers, the nickel route, make up 60
percent of the capacity being built (International Energy Agency, 2026g). thyssenkrupp
nucera, the leading European alkaline electrolyser maker, saw green hydrogen orders fall from
EUR 356 million to EUR 26 million in fiscal 2024/25 (thyssenkrupp nucera, 2025).

Nuclear power offers a slower but steadier pull, with 81 reactors under construction, 40 of
them in China, and a United States framework of October 2025 covering at least USD 80
billion of AP1000 reactors (Westinghouse Electric, 2025; World Nuclear Association, 2026b).
At the metal stage, the IEA puts nickel demand for hydrogen technologies at only 2.3 kt in
2025 and 5.2 kt in 2035 in its stated policies case, or 32 kt in its high demand case, while gas
turbine superalloy tonnage is not published (International Energy Agency, 2026b).

5.4       Aerospace

Aerospace is the growth engine of high performance metals, because every jet engine hot
section is made of nickel and cobalt superalloys that the supply chain struggles to melt and
forge fast enough.


5.4.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size

Fleet growth sets the long term path, as Airbus expects the passenger fleet to grow from
23,310 aircraft in 2025 to 45,550 in 2045, which requires 42,060 new aircraft, while Boeing
expects 43,625 deliveries on a wider definition (Airbus, 2026a; Boeing, 2026a). Engines grow
faster than aircraft, as CFM delivered 1,802 LEAP engines in 2025, up 28 percent (GE
Aerospace, 2026a; Safran, 2026a). The aftermarket adds profit, since aviation services grow
from USD 209 billion in 2026 to USD 422 billion in 2045 and materials drive over 85 percent
of off wing maintenance cost (Airbus, 2026b).

Defence adds a third stream, with military expenditure of USD 2,887 billion in 2025 and GE
delivering 635 defence engines against 490 a year earlier (GE Aerospace, 2026a; Stockholm
International Peace Research Institute, 2026). Although deliveries shift toward Asia, qualified
melting, forging and casting capacity stays in the United States, the United Kingdom, France
                                                                                                17
and Germany. The best measured size at the metal stage is cobalt, as the Cobalt Institute
counts 11,499 t of cobalt in aviation in 2025 within 20 kt used in superalloys, and the U.S.
Geological Survey reports that 51 percent of United States cobalt consumption goes into
superalloys, mainly for aircraft engines, while nickel alloy tonnage is not published (Cobalt
Institute, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026e).


5.4.2     Applications and Opportunities

Rotating parts set the highest barrier among the applications in Table 6. Disc and shaft billet is

5.5       Chemical Processing

Chemical processing is the traditional heartland of corrosion resistant alloys, but in Europe it
has become a maintenance market while new plants are built in Asia.


5.5.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size

Europe's chemical industry is shrinking in volume and running with spare capacity. EU27
chemical sales were about EUR 635 billion in 2024, but Europe's share of world chemical
sales fell from 21 percent in 2009 to 13 percent, while China's rose from 24 to 46 percent
(European Chemical Industry Council, 2025a, 2025b). The EU chemical production index fell
3.2 percent in 2025, and capacity utilisation was 75.0 percent in the third quarter of 2026
against a long term average of 81.3 percent (European Chemical Industry Council, 2026b;
Eurostat, 2026c). New capital follows growth to Asia, as BASF expects over 80 percent of
chemical industry growth to be in Asia Pacific by 2035 and opened its Zhanjiang Verbund site
in November 2025 (BASF, 2026). The closest measured size at the metal stage is EU apparent
consumption of nickel plate, sheet, strip and foil, 57.8 kt in 2024 across all end uses, of which
chemical processing holds an unmeasured share (Eurostat, 2025d).

5.6       Electronics and Electrical Engineering

Electronics has the largest headline numbers and the smallest metal volumes, because
booming chip and data centre markets use precision strip, foil and high purity alloys measured
in tonnes.


5.6.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size

The semiconductor boom is real but must be read carefully before it is translated into metal.
Semiconductor sales reached USD 795.6 billion in 2025, up 26.2 percent, and WSTS forecasts
USD 1.51 trillion for 2026 mainly because memory sales more than triple, so sales value rises
without a comparable rise in metal volume (World Semiconductor Trade Statistics, 2026).
Equipment spending is the closer indicator, and SEMI expects 300 mm fab equipment
spending of USD 149 billion in 2026 and USD 227 billion in 2029, with fabs in Taiwan,
Korea, China and increasingly the United States (SEMI, 2026a, 2026c). Germany's electrical
and electronics industry added EUR 112.2 billion of value in 2023 but showed flat real output
in 2025 (OECD, 2026; ZVEI, 2026). No public source measures FeNi or high purity alloy
tonnage for electronics, whose FeNi growth comes from electronics and electric drive sensors
rather than from engine alloys.

5.7       Medical Technology

Medical technology offers the steadiest growth of all seven industries, because ageing
populations rather than business cycles drive the hip and knee replacements made from cobalt
chromium, titanium and zirconium alloys.

5.7.1   Demand, Geography and Market Size

Joint replacement is the most measurable driver of implant metal demand, as in 2023 the
OECD average was 198 hip and 156 knee replacements per 100,000 people, and rates rose

6.3    Corridors and Dependencies

The geography of trade mirrors the geography of the industries that buy. German nickel bar
and wire go mainly to the United Kingdom and the United States, each with about USD 111
million in 2024, the engine corridors of section 5.4, while plate and strip go mainly to Austria,
Italy, China and the United States, where chemical and electronics fabricators work (United
Nations Statistics Division, 2026b, 2026c). Engine trade confirms the picture, with British
exports of turbojets and gas turbines at USD 39.8 bn in 2024, re exports included, and trade
policy now reshapes these corridors (United Nations Statistics Division, 2026e).


6.4    Trade Policy Reshapes Sourcing and Access

Trade policy now changes costs and market access faster than demand does, as the United
States raised its Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminium to 50 percent in 2025 and taxed the
                                                                                                          25
copper content of semifinished copper products at 50 percent from 1 August 2025 (The White
House, 2025). The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, definitive since 1 January
2026, covers ferrochrome and ferronickel but not the nickel products of HS chapter 75, so it
raises input costs without protecting nickel alloy mill products (Ernst & Young, 2025). The
Chinese licences, Congolese cobalt quota and Indonesian ore quota of chapter 4 add supply
risk, while the EU Critical Raw Materials Act lists nickel, cobalt and tungsten as strategic or
critical (European Parliament and Council, 2024; Indonesia Business Post, 2026; Ministry of
Commerce of the People's Republic of China, 2025a; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026e). These
measures reward producers that convert close to their customers and secure several sources of
each critical input, the setting in which chapter 7 projects demand to 2035.

9.     Conclusion

This study asked how supply concentration, conversion and industry needs shape the
opportunities in high performance materials through 2035, and its answer is that value lies in
conversion and qualification rather than in metal volume. Nickel is in surplus while cobalt,
tungsten and molybdenum are bound by quotas and licences, yet the countries that convert
metals into qualified bar, strip and tube earn the trade surplus, and cobalt is a cost risk rather
than a volume ceiling for superalloys.

The tier 1 model then locates demand, with energy technology and aerospace at P50 indices of
156 and 147 in 2035 and automotive flat. The most attractive measured opportunities are
therefore turbine remelt stock, engine billet and precision FeNi foil in the United States,
Europe and East Asia, followed by approval protected franchises, while solid oxide strip,
fusion magnet tape, additive powder and small reactor tubing are tier 2 options to monitor
against their signposts.

These findings have limits, because official statistics stop at the mill product and most indices
rest on activity drivers and team weights, not measured alloy tonnes. Within those limits the
decade to 2035 rewards producers that secure their inputs, qualify early with engine and
turbine makers and invest only where signed demand has arrived.

Appendices: Data, Sources, Calculations, and Assumptions

A1     Definitions and the stage ladder
The report uses Mt for million tonnes, kt for thousand tonnes, t for metric tonnes, GW for
gigawatts and bcm for billion cubic metres, and all prices are in current US dollars or euros of
the year stated. The stage ladder separates five rungs that are never added together: mine
output of contained metal, refined metal or intermediate such as nickel pig iron or cobalt
hydroxide, alloy melt, mill product such as strip, sheet, plate, bar and wire, and finished
component. Contained metal is kept apart from gross weight, actual output from capacity, and
commodity benchmarks such as the World Bank Pink Sheet from the selling price of qualified
alloy products.

A2     Outlook model, backtest and opportunity scoring
The tier 1 model of chapter 7 is fully reproducible from the repository, and this appendix
documents its formulas, parameters, backtest, simulation settings and the scoring rules of
chapter 8. Every value is a team estimate unless a source is given, and running python3
model/outlook_2035.py from the repository root rewrites data/clean/model_tier1_results.csv,
model_backtest.csv,          model_calibration.csv,             model_supply_overlay.csv,
model_tier2_register.csv, research/model/outlook_results.md and Figures 3 and 4 with
identical numbers, while the option --no-log skips the update of the source register (Product
Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).

A2.1 Formulas and simulation settings

For each industry j with components k, such as new engines and aftermarket in aerospace, the
2035 index is I(j) = 100 × Σ w(k) × A(k) × (1 + i(k))^10 × S(k) × exp(10 × (Δg(j) + σ(j) × ε)).
Here A is the activity ratio 2035 to 2025, i the annual trend of alloy mass per unit, S the ratio
of the high performance alloy share, w the component weight, Δg the backtest shift and σ the
backtest error carried into the range, with ε a standard normal draw. The shift is Δg = −λ × e,
where e is the mean annual growth error of the real backtest series of the industry, λ = 0.5 and
the shift is capped at 1 percentage point a year, while σ = κ × the root mean square growth
error of all series of the industry, with κ = 0.5 and a cap of 3 percentage points. A product
family index is the weighted sum of the industry indices with the weights of Table A1, and
cobalt tonnes multiply the 2025 base by the index and by the cobalt content ratio.

The simulation draws 10,000 values with the random seed 20261108 from triangular
distributions with the low, mode and high values of Table A1. Activity drivers are linked by a
Gaussian copula with a global cycle weight of 0.2 and an industry weight of 0.3, intensity and
                                                                                             45
share draws are independent, and component and product weights vary around their mode in a
Dirichlet distribution with concentration 40. All of these settings, λ, κ and both caps are team
assumptions, because the backtest windows are short and the test period contains the
pandemic.

A3      Data sources, product codes and reproducibility
All structured data were pulled with the project's fetch script, which stores raw files, writes
clean tables and logs every query with its date and URL in the source register of more than
1,000 entries. UN Comtrade data cover the product codes HS 7502, 7504, 7505, 7506, 7507,
8105, 8109, 7219, 7220, 7223, 7409, 7606, 8311 and 8411 for ten reporting countries from
2019 to 2024, and Eurostat PRODCOM data cover fifteen nickel, cobalt, stainless, copper and
aluminium product codes for the EU and five producer countries. USGS Mineral Commodity
Summaries 2026, the World Bank Pink Sheet, OECD STAN and ICIO, UNIDO INDSTAT for
Germany and the filings of 61 companies complete the evidence base.
                                                                                            52
The value range of Table 10 in chapter 6 is a team estimate that multiplies EU apparent
consumption in tonnes by the lower and the higher of the extra EU export and import unit
values of 2024, because the Eurostat production values fall below the value of the contained
nickel. Flat products give 57.8 kt × EUR 31.0 per kg = EUR 1.79 bn and 57.8 kt × EUR 47.8
per kg = EUR 2.76 bn, and tubes give 9.2 kt × EUR 26.1 per kg = EUR 0.24 bn and 9.2 kt ×
EUR 41.1 per kg = EUR 0.38 bn, while bars are not valued because their production cell is
confidential (Eurostat, 2025c, 2026d). The unit values are trade value divided by trade
quantity in PRODCOM DS-059358, so the range reflects the mix of grades traded across the
EU border rather than a list price.

A4     Limitations and further research
The study has three limitations that further research on this market should address. Official
statistics stop at the mill product and mix pure nickel with nickel alloys, so the market for
specific alloys can only be approximated, and a producer level survey or customs data at the
eight digit level would sharpen these estimates. The tier 1 model rests on published activity
drivers, but its intensity, share and product family weights are team assumptions, nickel alloy
tonnes by industry are not published, and the backtest windows are short, so the model should
be rerun when the IEA World Energy Outlook 2026, the next Airbus and Boeing outlooks and
new SEMI forecasts appear. Finally, the trade analysis covers ten large reporting countries, so
a world mirror data analysis that includes Russia, Sweden and Austria would complete the
picture of global corridors.
