## 3. Errors with corrections (ranked fixes, located)

### Fix 1. The headline priority 1 programme is priority 2 in the team's own base case (ch 2 paragraph 4, 8.1 paragraph 5, ch 9 paragraph 2, Table A5 memo rows)
* Statement: ch 2 says "As one vacuum melt programme, they reach priority 1 under three of four weightings", and ch 9 says the two rows are "best run as one vacuum melt programme". 8.1 says "the combined vacuum melt programme is priority 1 under three of four weightings".
* Problem: Table A5 "Memo: combined vacuum melt programme" scores 3.70, tier 2, at base weights. The "three of four" are the three alternative weightings (equal 3.75, barriers 20 percent proportional 3.77, equal cut 3.77). outlook_results.md "Priority 1 check" says the same. The report therefore recommends, as its single action, a programme that fails its own priority 1 threshold at its own weights. It does so because the programme takes the weakest supply input (Waspaloy, Sup 2). A board member who reads A5 will stop trusting the chapter.
* Correction: say it plainly in ch 2, 8.1 and ch 9: "At base weights the combined programme scores 3.70 and is priority 2. Its Alloy 718 core and turbine remelt stock are each priority 1 at 3.85, and the programme clears 3.75 only under alternative weights." Alternatively, define the programme as 718 plus remelt stock without the cobalt grades and show that it scores at or above 3.75. Either version is credible. The current wording is not. Impact: Storyline +0.2.

### Fix 2. The supply rule that demoted Waspaloy is not applied to turbine remelt stock and combustor sheet (8.1 paragraph 3 "with cobalt as a minor element", Table 17 row 1 Sup 3, Table 2 cobalt row, Table 6 row 4, A2.6)
* Problem: the row bundles VIM casting stock and combustor sheet. Common lead grades contain cobalt above the report's 10 percent threshold. Equiaxed IN939 has about 19 percent, Mar M247 about 10 percent, and IN738 and GTD 111 about 8.5 to 9.5 percent. On the sheet side, Haynes 188 is a cobalt base alloy at about 39 percent and Alloy 263 has about 20 percent. Table 2 uses Haynes 188 combustor sheet as the report's own example of a superalloy, and 4.2 calls cobalt in "turbine remelt stock" "hard to switch". Single crystal grades add about 3 to 6 percent rhenium, a by product with 81 t of world mine output (4.7.4) and a price history of extreme spikes. Hafnium, a by product of nuclear zirconium refining, is in most DS and SC grades. Under A4's "weakest input of the lead grades" rule, this row scores Sup 2 and falls to 3.70, priority 2. That is exactly what Table 15 shows under the Congo shock, which shows that the model already treats the row as cobalt exposed.
* Correction: split the row into "IGT and aero casting stock (nickel base, lead grades IN738, GTD 111, CMSX 4, Mar M247)" and "combustor sheet (HX, 230, 625, 188, 263)", apply the rule to each and add a rhenium row to A4 (score 3 when below 10 percent, with a licence or price note). Name the lead grade behind every Sup score in Table 17. Then restate priority 1. If only the 718 row survives, say so, because a single clear priority 1 is the stronger message. Impact: Model +0.1, Actionability +0.1, credibility with any metallurgist on the panel.

### Fix 3. Casting stock is not in the bar codes, and German plate and strip does not reach casting houses (Table 10 row 1 "In bar codes", 8.1 paragraph 3, Table 11 row 7502)
* Statement: "Buyers are turbine makers and casting houses ... which German plate and strip already reaches (United Nations Statistics Division, 2026d)."
* Problem: investment casters buy VIM remelt ingot and bar stock, which customs classify mostly as unwrought nickel alloy, HS 750220, not as 7505 bar and not as 7506 plate. Plate and strip exports go to fabricators and are no evidence of access to casting houses. The 7502 row in Table 11 and Germany's −736 m net trade also mix 750210 (pure, class 1) with 750220 (alloy ingot), so part of the "raw nickel" import may be alloy remelt stock.
* Correction: pull 750210 and 750220 separately for the ten reporters and mirror partners, put 750220 beside the superalloy family in Table 10 as its traded floor, and replace the plate and strip sentence with the 750220 corridors to the United States and the United Kingdom. Impact: Research +0.1, Model +0.1.

### Fix 4. The energy driver and base must cover three OEMs, the aftermarket and the cycle (7.1 paragraph 2, Table A1 "Gas turbine output ratio" and "Weights turbines, nuclear, other", Table 13 Energy row, A4 paragraph 2, 8.1 "Its weak point")
* Problems:
  (a) The data on the other two OEMs are public (section 1). Heavy duty capacity summed across the three is roughly 17 GW (Siemens Energy FY2024) plus 20 GW (GE Vernova Q3 2026) plus Mitsubishi, rising to about 30 plus 30 plus double by 2030. The team's mode of 1.58 for 2035 output is plausible as a 2030 capacity peak, but 2035 output is a different quantity.
  (b) Gas turbine orders are among the most cyclical in capital goods. The 2001 to 2003 US order collapse and the 2017 to 2019 heavy duty slump both followed a capacity build of this kind. The 1.00 low already allows for this, but the text should say that 2035 may be a post peak year.
  (c) Hot gas path replacement on the installed fleet (blades, vanes and combustor parts replaced on 24k to 32k hour intervals, plus repair coupons) is a large and stable share of IGT superalloy use and is not modelled. The "other equipment" component (0.20) uses electricity demand, not the installed fleet.
  (d) Table 13 gives Energy a 2025 base of 8.5 kt Co, which is the whole non aviation remainder (20.0 minus 11.5). Table A1 assigns energy a family weight of 0.300, or 6.0 kt, and assigns 2.5 kt to cars, oil and gas and chemical. Use one figure.
* Correction: sum the three OEMs (GE Vernova webcast transcript July 2026, MHI FY2025 IR, Siemens Energy CMD 2025), model new units plus fleet aftermarket, relabel the base as 6.0 kt (or justify 8.5), and delete the "not available" sentence in A4. Impact: Model +0.2, Research +0.1.

### Fix 6. Qualification realism in the roadmap (Table 18 initiatives 1 and 4, A7 Table A14, A8 kill criterion 1)
* Initiative 1 qualifies 718 billet in 2026 to 2028 and adds VAR, ESR, conversion and inspection in 2029 to 2035. Rotating grade approvals freeze the melt route per furnace, so every new VAR or ESR furnace needs its own approval (furnace addition, characterisation heats, OEM audit), typically 12 to 24 months. Revenue from the added capacity therefore starts around 2031, not 2029. Also, a 2028 qualification for premium quality rotating 718 started in 2026 is optimistic. GE's "several years" (5.4.2) points to 2029 or 2030 for first rotating part approval. Non rotating 718 bar (cases, shafts, fasteners) to AMS 5662 and 5663 qualifies faster and is the realistic first revenue.
* The 1.5 kt gate by Q4 2027 is therefore unlikely to be met by signed rotating volume, and the A8 kill criterion would end the programme on a timing artefact. Make the gate "1.5 kt signed or in qualification, of which at least 0.5 kt non rotating".
* Nuclear: "Hold ASME and RCC-M approvals" should read ASME Section III NCA 3800 Quality System Certificate (material organisation), 10 CFR 50 Appendix B and NQA 1, plus RCC-M M4105 for EDF and Framatome Valinox programmes. Note also that the 40 Chinese reactors under construction take tube mainly from domestic mills (Baosteel, Jiuli), so the open Western 690 market is smaller than the reactor count suggests.
* Medical: name ISO 5832 4 and 5832 12 beside F75 and F1537 (A14 says only "ISO equivalents"), and add the device master file route.
* Aerospace gate (A14): name the material specs a buyer would ask for first (AMS 5662 and 5663 for 718 bar and forgings, AMS 5596 for 718 sheet, AMS 5536 for HX sheet) and the OEM specifications (GE, RR RRMS, PWA) layered on top.
* Impact: Actionability +0.2.
