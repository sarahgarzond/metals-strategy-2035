1.     Introduction
For centuries, metals have been among the essential materials on which human societies are
built, enabling the construction of cities, transport systems, power networks, machinery and
technologies that support modern economies. Their uses have expanded with each wave of
industrial development, and today they remain central to established industries as well as
emerging technologies. This enduring role is reflected in the sector’s economic scale:
according to McKinsey & Company’s Global Materials Perspective, the global metals and
mining industry generated approximately US$3.0 trillion in revenue in 2024, after a decline of
6 percent, and grew by only about 1 percent in 2025 (McKinsey & Company, 2025, 2026).

Within this broad market, high-performance alloys serve applications where ordinary materials
may not meet the required operating conditions. These engineered materials combine metals
such as nickel, cobalt, titanium, iron and chromium to provide properties such as strength at
high temperatures, corrosion resistance, oxidation resistance or reliable performance under
pressure. Nickel- and cobalt-based superalloys, titanium alloys and corrosion-resistant
specialty steels are examples. Demand comes from aerospace and defence, power generation,
chemical processing, oil and gas, medical technology and other industries with demanding
technical requirements. The production and availability of metals including nickel, cobalt,
chromium, molybdenum, titanium, tungsten, zirconium, copper, aluminium and manganese
therefore provide important context for understanding this specialist market.
The outlook for high-performance alloys to 2035 is positive, although growth is likely to differ
across alloy types and applications. One market estimate places the segment at US$13.6
billion in 2025 and projects it to reach US$19.0 billion by 2034 (The Insight Partners, 2025),
although other providers start as low as US$10.2 billion because they draw the boundary of
the segment differently (IMARC Group, 2025), while broader industry analysis points to
continued demand from aerospace, defence, energy and other industrial uses. This growth will
depend on the pace of investment and technology adoption, as well as the cost and availability
of alloying metals. The strongest prospects are likely to be in applications where a particular
combination of material properties, reliability and qualification requirements creates lasting
value for customers.

2.     Report Objective and Scope
This project studies selected metals that are essential to high performance alloys and follows
them from the mine to the industrial applications that depend on them, in order to assess how
their markets may develop through 2035. It asks one central question: how do the
concentration of metal supply, the conversion of metals into qualified product forms and the
needs of seven industries determine where the most attractive opportunities in high
                                                                                              4
performance materials will lie through 2035? Four subsidiary questions structure the answer,
and each maps onto one part of the report. The first asks where the ten metals are mined and
refined, how exposed that supply is to concentration and export policy, and in which qualified
forms the metals reach industry. The second asks which industries and regions drive demand
for these forms today and how large the measurable market is. The third asks how large
demand could become by 2035, how certain that estimate is and which emerging applications
could add to it, and the fourth asks where supply security, growth and entry barriers combine
into the most attractive opportunities.

The study covers ten metals: nickel, cobalt, chromium, molybdenum, titanium, tungsten,
zirconium, copper, aluminium, and manganese. It examines seven industries: automotive, oil
and gas, energy and environmental technology, aerospace, chemical processing, electronics
and electrical engineering, and medical technology. The study uses the latest official data
available in November 2026. It covers the global market and compares Europe, North
America, and Asia. The outlook quantifies established uses and evaluates emerging uses
separately because official statistics do not yet capture them.
The report argues that much of the value in this market comes from turning metals into
qualified products. Nickel is in surplus, while export policies restrict supplies of cobalt,
tungsten, and molybdenum. Countries that make qualified bar, strip, and tube earn trade
surpluses on those products that mining countries do not.

Measured in tonnes, the largest growing pool is superalloy bar, billet and remelt stock for
engines and turbines. It rises from about 193 kt in 2025 to 248 kt in 2035, worth EUR 6.3 to
11.7 bn (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a). By index, electronics grows fastest at 141,
ahead of aerospace and medical technology at 136, energy at 132 and automotive at 102
(Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a). Electronics tracks fab capacity with unmeasured alloy
volume, so it yields a priority 2 position in fab gas strip and tube. Priority 1 goes to turbine
remelt stock and sheet and to Alloy 718 engine bar, both at 3.85, for buyers in the United
States and Europe. As one vacuum melt programme, they reach priority 1 under three of four
weightings (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).

The ranking is conditional, since a tighter Congo cobalt quota or a severe aerospace downturn
each moves one of them to priority 2 (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a). Priority 2 covers
engine bar in all grades, CoCr implant bar, Alloy 690 and additive powder, while FeNi36 mask
foil and NO96 strip remain options. Producers should stop investing in diesel only parts, new
European base chemical plants, unfunded electrolyser capacity and captive zirconium
cladding.

3.      Introduction to the Analytical Framework and Methodology
The study follows the seven links of the metal value chain: material, product form,
application, industry, geography, market size, and growth. Table 1 shows which chapters cover
each link. Figures are compared only at the same stage, so contained metal is kept apart from
gross weight and output from capacity. Mine output, alloy melt, mill products, and industry
revenue are reported separately.
Structured data from the USGS, UN Comtrade, Eurostat PRODCOM, the World Bank, OECD,
UNIDO, and the IEA form the base. Associations, official outlooks, and 61 company profiles
add detail, and commercial reports serve only as a cross-check. Key figures are checked
against a second source where one exists. The source register records each value's unit, stage,
type, source, and formula and flags single-source figures.

share of units using a high-performance alloy. Activity measures such as engine deliveries, gas

and with earlier official outlooks. A Monte Carlo simulation gives the 10th, 50th, and 90th
percentiles of 2035 demand, and a supply analysis tests export quotas and licensing changes.

4.1      Nickel

Indonesia has become the largest source of nickel within a decade. The USGS estimates 2025
mine output at 3.90 Mt of contained nickel, with 2.60 Mt, or about two thirds, from Indonesia.
The IEA estimates 4.21 Mt, with Indonesia at 63 percent of mining and 45 percent of refining,
so both show concentrated supply (International Energy Agency, 2026c; U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026g).
                                                                                              8
Primary nickel output reached 3.875 Mt against 3.577 Mt of use, and just over half of Chinese
and Indonesian supply was nickel pig iron for stainless steel (International Nickel Study
Group, 2025, 2026). Vacuum-melted alloys need class 1 nickel, which is widely available,
because the Chinese share of open LME tonnage rose from 47 to about 68 percent in the first
nine months of 2025. The real constraint is approved feedstock from sources such as Vale in
Canada and Glencore Nikkelverk in Norway (International Nickel Study Group, 2025; United
Nations Statistics Division, 2026a). US and UK sanctions bar Russian metal produced from 13
April 2024 from US imports and exchange warrants (U.S. Department of the Treasury, 2024).
The surplus explains the falling price, from a World Bank average of USD 25,834 per tonne in
2022 to USD 15,162 in 2025 (World Bank, 2026a). Indonesia first cut its 2026 ore quota to
260 to 270 Mt, then raised it to 290 to 300 Mt, and the International Nickel Study Group cut
its 2026 surplus forecast to 19 kt, after 299 kt in 2025. The quota is therefore the main swing
factor for price (Argus Media, 2026a; Indonesia Business Post, 2026; International Nickel
Study Group, 2026).

Stainless steel uses most nickel, with melt shop output of 64.2 Mt gross in 2025. The IEA
expects nickel demand outside energy technologies, including stainless steel and alloys, to rise
from 2,906 kt in 2025 to 3,255 kt in 2035 (International Energy Agency, 2026c;
worldstainless, 2026).

Nickel alloys reach industry as strip, sheet, plate, bar, wire, and tube. In 2024, Germany
exported 17,775 t of nickel plate, sheet, and strip worth USD 578 million, about USD 32,500
per tonne or 1.9 times the nickel price (Eurostat, 2026a; United Nations Statistics Division,
2026d). That ratio overstates conversion value, because alloys contain other metals. Alloy C-
276, with about 57 percent nickel, 16 percent molybdenum, and 16 percent chromium, held
metal worth about USD 19,000 per tonne before tungsten. The team estimates the export unit
value at about 1.7 times that metal content (Haynes International, 2023b; U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026c, 2026f; World Bank, 2026a).
Producers pass metal costs on through surcharges or index clauses, as ATI does for most of its
70 million pounds of nickel, and Carpenter for sales outside the 43 percent under firm prices
(ATI, 2026a; Carpenter Technology, 2026). Margins therefore depend on base prices. Special
Metals raised its Huntington base prices eight times from April 2021 to February 2024, by 5 to
20 percent each time (Special Metals Corporation, 2024). Form and qualification explain why
buyers pay above metal value, while cobalt adds a sharper supply risk.

4.2    Cobalt
Export restrictions and concentrated mining make cobalt scarce. The USGS estimates 2025
mine output at 310 kt of contained cobalt. The Democratic Republic of the Congo mined about
230 kt, which the USGS puts at 73 percent of the world total. China refined 78.6 percent of the
world's 240 kt of refined cobalt (Cobalt Institute, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026d).

In February 2025, Congo banned cobalt exports, then set an annual quota of up to 96,600 t for
2026 and 2027. CMOC mined 117,549 t in 2025 and received an export allowance of 31,200 t.
European cobalt metal prices rose 130 percent in 2025 (Cobalt Institute, 2026; Mining Weekly,
2026; Mining.com, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026d). The IEA expects 2035 mine supply
of 225 kt against demand of 300 to 322 kt, a lasting gap (International Energy Agency, 2026c).
Superalloys use a small share of cobalt but need high-purity metal. In 2025, batteries used 206
kt and superalloys 20 kt. A few Western refineries supply most of the superalloy grade (Cobalt
Institute, 2026). Its uses in turbine remelt stock, cobalt-chromium implants, and wear-resistant
alloys are hard to switch, so a quota set in Kinshasa can disrupt production in Toulouse and
Cincinnati.


4.3    Chromium
Chromium gives corrosion-resistant and heat-resistant alloys their protective oxide layer, but it
is mined and smelted in different countries. World chromite ore output reached an estimated
51 Mt gross weight in 2025. The USGS puts South Africa at about 23 Mt, while the
International Chromium Development Association reports 25.2 Mt on a different definition
(International Chromium Development Association, n.d.; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026c).
China has little chromite but smelts 9.9 Mt of ferrochrome, mostly for stainless steel. The
United States imports 79 percent of its chromium needs (International Chromium
Development Association, n.d.; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026c; worldstainless, 2026).

Vacuum-melted nickel alloys need purer inputs, such as low-carbon ferrochrome and
aluminothermic chromium metal. The US chromium metal price rose to an estimated USD
5.90 per pound in 2025, while ferrochrome fell (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026c). AMG
Chrome in the United Kingdom supplies low-gas chromium metal for single-crystal turbine
blades. A vacuum melt shop can stop if it loses this qualified source (AMG Critical Materials
N.V., n.d.).


4.4    Molybdenum
Molybdenum helps alloys resist hot acids and seawater, in amounts that depend on the grade.
One tonne of 316L stainless steel contains about 21 kg, but Alloy C-276 holds about 160 kg
and Alloy 625 about 90 kg (ATI, n.d.; Haynes International, 2023b; Outokumpu, 2025).
                                                                                             10
Much of the world's molybdenum is a by-product of copper mining, so supply responds slowly
to price. The USGS estimates 2025 mine output at 260 kt, while the International
Molybdenum Association reports 672.6 million pounds, about 305 kt, with almost all of the
gap in China (International Molybdenum Association, 2026a; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026f).
According to the USGS, only China and the United States produce from primary molybdenum
mines. Since February 2025, China has required licences for molybdenum powder exports.
The oxide and ferromolybdenum that melt shops buy are excluded (Ministry of Commerce of
the People's Republic of China, 2025a; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026f). The US oxide price
rose 7 percent to about USD 51 per kg in 2025, while European use fell 7 percent with the
chemical slowdown (International Molybdenum Association, 2026a; U.S. Geological Survey,
2026f).


4.5    Titanium
Titanium replaces nickel alloys in lighter and cooler aerospace parts. In 2025, world ilmenite
and rutile output reached 9.8 Mt as TiO2, mostly for pigment, but titanium sponge output was
only 370 kt (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026i, 2026j).
China produced about 260 kt of sponge and held 320 kt of the world's 470 kt of capacity.
Western aircraft engines use other qualified sources, and the United States has no operating
sponge plant (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026i). Russia produced about 25 kt of sponge in 2025
and shipped USD 715 million of titanium to the ten reporters in 2024, and its VSMPO-
AVISMA calls itself the leading titanium supplier for aviation (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026i;
United Nations Statistics Division, 2026a; VSMPO-AVISMA Corporation, n.d.).

The scarce forms are aircraft-grade sponge and the ingot, bar, and plate made from it. Osaka
Titanium is raising aircraft-grade capacity from 40 kt to 50 kt by fiscal 2027. Kobe Steel sells
titanium castings and forgings for aircraft, and ATI reports lead times of about 20 months for
premium titanium. Europe makes no sponge but sold 22.6 kt of wrought titanium in 2024 and
imported 31.7 kt (ATI, 2026b; Eurostat, 2024; Kobe Steel Ltd., 2026; Osaka Titanium
Technologies, 2025). Nickel superalloys face the same shortage of qualified melting capacity.


4.6    Tungsten
China controls most of the world's tungsten supply. In 2025, mines produced an estimated 85
kt of contained tungsten, of which China produced about 67 kt, or 79 percent (U.S. Geological
Survey, 2026k). China's first 2025 mining quota covered 58,000 t of concentrate. Since
February 2025, it has required export licences for ammonium paratungstate, tungsten oxides,
unsintered carbide, and specified tungsten alloys. Sintered carbide tools and tungsten wire
remain outside these controls (Ministry of Commerce of the People's Republic of China,
2025a, 2025b; Ministry of Natural Resources, 2025).
                                                                                               11
Carbide for cutting and mining tools takes 65 percent of first use, and steels and superalloys
take 14 percent (International Tungsten Industry Association, n.d.). In superalloys, tungsten
strengthens cast turbine parts and heat-resistant sheet, so export licences can affect gas turbine
output. Sandvik's powder business more than doubled orders and revenue in the third quarter
of 2026 as tungsten prices rose (Sandvik, 2026).


4.7     Other Selected Metals
Zirconium and the minor superalloy elements serve narrow but demanding uses, while copper,
aluminium, and manganese have large markets but small high-performance roles.

4.7.1   Zirconium

World output of zirconium concentrates reached about 1.2 Mt gross weight in 2025, led by
Australia with 400 kt. Most goes into ceramics, foundry sand, and refractories, and the metal
market is far smaller (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026l). Fuel makers and integrated producers
such as ATI control their own nuclear cladding supply, so the open market covers mainly plate
and tube for acid service (ATI, 2026a).

4.7.2   Copper

Copper links high-performance materials to electrification, with 2025 mine production of
about 23.2 Mt. Flat-rolled products take 11 percent of first use, against 63 percent for wire.
Copper averaged USD 9,947 per tonne in 2025 and set records above USD 14,500 in 2026
(International Copper Study Group, 2025, 2026; International Energy Agency, 2026c; The
Oregon Group, 2026; World Bank, 2026b). Under current policies, the IEA expects output
from current mines to fall to 20.7 Mt by 2035, while demand rises from 27.8 Mt to 32.4 Mt.
That implies a primary supply deficit of about 25 percent (International Energy Agency,
2026b, 2026c). Specialized forms include copper-nickel and high-conductivity strip, such as
the busbars in section 5.6.


4.7.3   Aluminium and Manganese
Aluminium can replace heavier alloys where temperatures and corrosion allow. World primary
output reached about 74 Mt in 2025, 61 percent of it in China. European primary output has
fallen to about 955 kt, against demand of about 9 Mt (European Aluminium, n.d.; Federation
of Aluminium Consumers in Europe, 2024; International Aluminium Institute, 2026; U.S.
Geological Survey, 2026b).
Manganese demand follows carbon steel output. Mines produced about 20 Mt of contained
manganese in 2025, about 81 percent from South Africa, Gabon, Ghana, and Australia. The
ore price fell to about USD 4.5 per metric ton unit, so supply risk and growth potential for
                                                                                             12
alloy producers are limited (International Manganese Institute, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey,
2026e).

4.7.4   Additional Metals

Iron matters in FeNi36 for display masks and FeNi42 for lead frames, whose value lies mainly
in rolling to tight specifications. Alloy 718 contains about 5 percent niobium. Brazil mines
about 93 percent of the world's niobium, and CBMM sells it as vacuum-grade nickel-niobium.
Single-crystal turbine blades contain rhenium, of which mines produced only about 81 t in
2025 (CBMM, n.d.; RTX, 2026; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026a, 2026h). These supply facts
frame the industry demand in chapter 5.


5.      Industry Needs: Demand, Geography and Market Size
Each industry section starts with market size and geography and then covers applications and
product forms. The first subsection ends with the best measure at a named stage, and chapter 7
uses a team index where tonnage is missing (indicators in appendix A5). To avoid double
counting, electrolyzers and fuel cells sit under energy, process vessels under chemical
processing, and sensors under electronics.


5.1     Automotive
Electrification cuts demand for the heat-resistant nickel alloys of combustion vehicles, because
electric cars need no turbochargers, exhaust sensors, or glow plugs.


5.1.1   Demand, Geography and Market Size
The world produced 96.4 million motor vehicles in 2025, 34.5 million of them in China. EU
car registrations reached 10.8 million, 17 percent below the 2019 level (European Automobile
Manufacturers' Association, 2026a; International Organization of Motor Vehicle
Manufacturers, 2026).
Over 20 million electric cars were sold in 2025, about a quarter of the market. The IEA
expects about half of global sales by 2035 and more than 90 percent in China and the EU
(International Energy Agency, 2026d). Batteries do not replace the lost engine alloy nickel,
because lithium iron phosphate cathodes contain none and held over 55 percent of the 2025
market (International Energy Agency, 2026d).
European policy may slow the shift. In October 2026, two European Parliament committees
backed a 95 percent cut in new car CO2 emissions by 2035, or 87.5 percent with low-carbon
steel credits, ahead of a December plenary vote (electrive, 2026).
                                                                                                13
The best nickel measure covers battery cathodes, at 439 kt in 2025 and 1,294 kt to 1,536 kt in
2035, while declining engine alloy tonnage is not published (International Energy Agency,
2026c).

5.1.2     Applications and Opportunities
Hybrid and gasoline engines keep turbochargers in production for another decade, after close
to 50 million units in 2025. Garrett expects output to fall toward its 2022 level by 2030 and the
turbo share of gasoline engines to peak at 54 percent in 2032 (Garrett Motion, 2026).
BorgWarner won three gasoline turbocharger programs in October 2026, starting in 2027
(BorgWarner, 2026).

5.2       Oil and Gas
Oil and gas is a stable market for high-performance metals, as investment moves from oil to
gas. Sour gas, deepwater fields, and liquefied natural gas (LNG) need alloys that ordinary steel
cannot replace.

5.2.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size
Global upstream oil and gas investment fell from USD 625 billion in 2019 to USD 542 billion
in 2025 and was about USD 546 billion in 2026 (International Energy Agency, 2026i, 2026k).
                                                                                               14
In 2025, about 40 percent of upstream spending went to slowing the decline of existing fields
(International Energy Agency, 2025b).

North America remained the largest region, at USD 165 billion in 2026 against USD 219
billion in 2019. Middle East investment rose from USD 75 billion to USD 93 billion, with gas
roughly doubling to USD 45.4 billion, and Central and South America grew fastest, from USD
42 billion to USD 68 billion (International Energy Agency, 2026k). Under current policies,
LNG trade could rise from 560 bcm in 2024 to 880 bcm in 2035 (International Energy Agency,
2025c, 2026k). Alloy and clad pipe tonnes are not published, so investment is the best
measure.


5.2.2     Applications and Opportunities
Customer approval is the main entry barrier. ISO 15156 sets which alloys suit sour service, but
producers still need NORSOK M-650 qualification and operator approval for each product
form (International Organization for Standardization, 2020; Standards Norway, 2011). Orders
flow through the vendor lists of EPC contractors and subsea firms such as SLB and

5.3       Energy and Environmental Technology

Energy technology shows two trends: gas turbine makers struggle to fill orders, while many
hydrogen projects have yet to place any.

5.3.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size
Gas turbine orders grow as data centres need power and grids need stability. Siemens Energy
is sold out until fiscal 2028 and plans to raise annual gas turbine capacity from about 17 GW
in fiscal 2024 to more than 30 GW (Siemens Energy, 2025). Gas-fired power investment rose
from USD 64 billion in 2019 to USD 112 billion in 2025, and data centres could use about 945
TWh in 2030, twice their 2024 use (International Energy Agency, 2025a, 2026k).
Hydrogen demand for alloys moves far more slowly. Low-emissions hydrogen output was
about 1 Mt in 2025, against total demand above 100 Mt, and the 2030 pipeline fell to 27 Mt
(International Energy Agency, 2026e, 2026f). The IEA lists 4.3 GW of operating electrolysis
and 19.3 GW at final investment decision or under construction, 60 percent of it nickel-using
alkaline units (International Energy Agency, 2026h). Green hydrogen orders at thyssenkrupp
nucera fell from EUR 356 million to EUR 26 million in fiscal 2024/25 (thyssenkrupp nucera,
2025). The IEA puts hydrogen nickel use at 2.3 kt in 2025 and 5.2 kt to 32 kt in 2035
(International Energy Agency, 2026c).
Nuclear power offers steadier but slower demand. Of 81 reactors under construction, 40 are in
China, and an October 2025 US framework covers at least USD 80 billion of AP1000 reactors
(K&L Gates, 2025; World Nuclear Association, 2026b). The superalloy tonnage used in gas
turbines is not published.

5.4       Aerospace

Aerospace is a major growth source for high-performance metals. Jet engine hot sections use
nickel and cobalt superalloys, which suppliers cannot melt and forge fast enough.

5.4.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size
Airbus expects the passenger fleet to grow from 23,310 aircraft in 2025 to 45,550 in 2045,

5.5       Chemical Processing

5.5.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size

Europe's chemical industry produces less and has spare capacity, with EU27 sales of about

5.7       Medical Technology
Medical technology offers the steadiest growth of the seven industries. Aging populations
drive demand for cobalt-chromium, titanium, and zirconium implants, largely independent of
business cycles.

5.7.1     Demand, Geography and Market Size
Joint replacements are the most measurable driver of implant metal. In 2023, the OECD

2023, excluding the United States and Japan (OECD, 2025).

6.3      Corridors and Dependencies
The main trade routes follow the buyers. In 2024, Germany exported about USD 111 to 112
million of nickel bar and wire each to the United Kingdom and the United States, both key
engine markets. German plate and strip go mainly to Austria, Italy, China, and the United
States, where chemical and electronics fabricators operate (United Nations Statistics Division,

including re-exports (United Nations Statistics Division, 2026g). The United States exported
the most alloy bar, 26.4 kt at USD 44.2 per kg, against 10.4 kt at USD 34.4 per kg from
Germany (United Nations Statistics Division, 2026c). These corridors are exposed to the trade
policies described next.

6.4      Trade Policy Reshapes Sourcing and Access

Trade policy can change costs and access quickly. In 2025, the United States raised Section
232 tariffs on steel and aluminium to 50 percent and, from 1 August, taxed the copper content
of copper semis at 50 percent (The White House, 2025a). The general US duty on nickel mill
products (HS 7505 to 7507) is 2 to 3 percent (United States International Trade Commission,
2026).
Additional US tariffs on EU goods have changed several times since 2025. The EU and US
joint statement of 21 August 2025 capped the combined tariff on most EU goods at 15 percent,
general duty included (The White House, 2025b). In February 2026, the United States ended
the reciprocal tariffs and applied a 10 percent surcharge for 150 days (Executive Office of the
President, 2026a, 2026b). Since 24 July 2026, a Section 301 action sets the combined EU rate
at 10 percent where the general duty is lower, and its exemption lists could not be checked for
HS 7505 to 7507 (Office of the United States Trade Representative, 2026). EU mills supplying
US forgers may therefore pay up to 10 percent that US mills such as ATI and Carpenter avoid.
The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026.
It covers ferrochrome and ferronickel but not the nickel products of HS chapter 75, so it raises
input costs without protecting alloy mill products (Ernst & Young, 2025). The EU Critical
Raw Materials Act lists nickel, cobalt, and tungsten as strategic or critical (European
Parliament and Council, 2024). With the quotas and licences of chapter 4, these measures
                                                                                              25
favour conversion near customers and several qualified sources per critical input, conditions
that chapter 7 carries into the growth scenarios.

7.1     Growth Model and Scenario Assumptions
Tier 1 models demand as D = A × I × S, with activity A, alloy mass per unit I and the share S
of units using a high performance alloy. Each factor is a 2035 to 2025 ratio, so the index is 100
× (1 + g)^10 with g the growth rate a year. P10, P50 and P90 come from 10,000 draws,
calibrated to 20.0 kt of superalloy cobalt in 2025, with all parameters in Table A1 (Cobalt
Institute, 2026).
Energy follows gas turbine output (ratio 1.00 to 2.00, Siemens Energy alone) and nuclear
(1.05 to 1.45). Electronics follows SEMI fab capacity, not alloy tonnes. Medical follows hip
and knee procedures at 3.0 to 4.1 percent a year. Chemical activity moves −1.0 to 2.5 percent a
year. Oil and gas follows upstream investment (0.80 to 1.12) and LNG (1.15 to 1.51).
Automotive follows an electric share of 40 to 62 percent in 2035 (European Chemical Industry
Council, 2026b; International Energy Agency, 2025c, 2026d; OECD, 2025; SEMI, 2026a;
Siemens Energy, 2025).

Aerospace combines the aftermarket at 3.0 to 4.2 percent a year with new engine deliveries.
The outlooks expect 2,103 to 2,181 deliveries a year to 2045 against 1,393 in 2025, a ratio of
1.25 to 1.76 by 2035. The 2025 base already holds the LEAP ramp to 1,802 engines, so P50
growth of 3.7 percent a year is not pessimism (Airbus, 2026c; Boeing, 2026b; GE Aerospace,
2026a; Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).
The model haircuts guidance, which tends to run ahead of outcomes. For aerospace, its mode
is the mean (0.982) of three points, with a median draw of 0.96. Boeing reached a 737 rate of
42 a month in Q4 2025 against a 2026 plan of 47 (0.894). Airbus runs at about 856 of 870
guided deliveries (0.983), and LEAP beat its guidance (1.068) (Airbus, 2026b; Boeing, 2026a;
GE Aerospace, 2026c; Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a). The IEA upstream ratio of 0.951
mixes price bases and is excluded for aerospace, but it sets the other haircuts (median 0.94).
The backtest in A2.3 widens the ranges (International Energy Agency, 2026k).

9.     Conclusion
The study asked how supply, trade and industrial use of selected metals shape opportunities
through 2035. By volume, superalloy bar, billet and remelt stock leads, growing from 193 kt to
248 kt by 2035, worth EUR 6.3 to 11.7 bn (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a). Value comes
from conversion into qualified products, while quotas raise cobalt and tungsten costs more
than they cap output.
By index, electronics grows fastest at 141, but its alloy volume is unmeasured, so it yields a
priority 2 position in fab gas strip and tube. Priority 1 goes to turbine remelt stock and sheet
and to Alloy 718 engine bar for engine and turbine makers in the United States and Europe,
best run as one vacuum melt programme. Both depend on cobalt supply and aerospace
volumes (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).
Team weights carry the estimates, since official data stop at mill products. Producers should
therefore secure inputs, qualify early with engine and turbine makers, and add remelt capacity
after customers sign.

Appendices: Data, Sources, Calculations, and Assumptions

A1     Definitions and the stage ladder
Mt means million tonnes, kt means thousand tonnes, and t means metric tonnes. GW means
gigawatts, and bcm means billion cubic meters. Prices are in current U.S. dollars or euros for
the year stated.
The value chain has five separate stages: contained metal in mine output, refined metal or
intermediates, alloy melt, mill products, and finished components. Examples of intermediates
are nickel pig iron and cobalt hydroxide. Mill products include strip, sheet, plate, bar, and
wire. The study reports each stage separately. It also separates contained metal from gross
weight, actual output from capacity, and commodity benchmark prices such as the World Bank
Pink Sheet from prices for qualified alloy products.

A2     Outlook model, backtest and opportunity scoring
This appendix documents the formulas, parameters, tests and scoring rules behind chapters 7
and 8. Values are team estimates unless a source is listed. Running python3
model/outlook_2035.py from the repository root reproduces every result in chapters 7 and 8,
writes data/clean/model_*.csv and research/model/outlook_results.md, and                  calls
model/scoring.py for the opportunity scores (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).


A2.1 Formulas and simulation settings
The model divides each industry j into components k, for example new engines and the
aftermarket in aerospace. The 2035 index is I(j) = 100 × Σ w(k) × A(k) × H(k) × (1 + i(k))^10
                                                                                               43
× S(k) × exp(10 × σ(j) × ε). A is the activity ratio from 2025 to 2035, H the optimism haircut
on drivers taken from official guidance, i the annual trend in alloy mass per unit, S the ratio of
the share using high performance alloy and w the component weight. σ carries the backtest
error into the range, and ε is a standard normal draw.
No trend shift is added to the growth rate. The drivers are official guidance and scenarios, so
adding a backtest trend error on top would count the same optimism twice. σ equals half the
root mean square growth error of the backtest series, capped at 3 percentage points a year.

A product family index is the weighted sum of the industry indices, with the weights in Table
A1. Cobalt tonnes are the 2025 base times the index and the cobalt content ratio. Paths
between 2025 and 2035 grow at a constant compound rate for each component.
The simulation uses 10,000 draws and random seed 20261108, with triangular distributions on
the low, mode and high values of Table A1. A Gaussian copula links the activity drivers with a
global cycle weight of 0.2 and an industry weight of 0.3. Intensity and share are drawn
independently. Component and family weights vary in a Dirichlet distribution with
concentration 40, or 8 for the FeNi family because its weights rest on less evidence. These
settings are team assumptions, because the backtest windows are short and include the
pandemic.

A2.4 Product family tonnes, values and shock arithmetic
The family tonnes in Table 14 are team estimates at the mill product stage in gross weight, and
they overlap, so they are never summed. Superalloy bar, billet and remelt stock divides the
20.0 kt of cobalt in superalloys by a virgin cobalt content of 4 to 15 percent and multiplies by
the 75 to 95 percent that leaves the mill as bar, billet or remelt stock. This gives 193 kt at P50
in 2025, with a P10 to P90 range of 138 to 279 kt (Cobalt Institute, 2026; Product Strategy
2035 Team, 2026a).
Two cross checks bound this figure. ATI's fifth VIM furnace at Monroe adds about 9 kt a year
from 2027, 5 percent of the P50 (Argus Media, 2026b). China's reported output of 64.5 kt in
2025, taken from a market report and used only for triangulation, equals 33 percent of the P50
(Zhiyan Consulting, 2026).
The other families start from measured trade or consumption. Long products use the HS
750512 and 750522 alloy only exports of eight reporters in 2024, 80.3 kt or 97 percent of all
HS 7505 exports, times 1.0 to 2.5 for output that never crosses a border. Flat products divide
the EU27 apparent consumption of 57.8 kt by an EU share of world use of 12 to 30 percent,
and tubes do the same with 9.2 kt. The HS 6 alloy only floors of 38.2 kt for flat products (85
percent of HS 7506) and 15.3 kt for tubes (87 percent of HS 7507) sit below both results
(Eurostat, 2026c; Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a; United Nations Statistics Division,
2026c). FeNi foil is not estimable, because no foil tonnes are published.
Values multiply the P50 tonnes by the 2024 extra EU unit values of EUR 25.4 to 33.4 per kg
for bar, 31.0 to 47.8 for flat products and 26.1 to 41.1 for tubes. The superalloy family adds a
premium of 1.00 to 1.41 over bar (Eurostat, 2025; Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a). The
2025 central ranges are EUR 4.9 to 9.1 bn for superalloys, 3.3 to 4.4 bn for long products, 8.7
to 13.5 bn for flat products and 1.2 to 1.8 bn for tubes. The 2035 values keep the 2024 unit
values constant. At superalloy values, the 1.5 kt gate of Table 18 equals EUR 38 to 71 m of
annual sales, or 0.8 percent of the 2025 family (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).
The cost effects behind the shock cases of Table 15 are team arithmetic. At 8 percent virgin
cobalt, a 25 percent rise in the cobalt price adds about USD 746 per tonne of superalloy and
USD 5,595 per tonne of CoCr. A doubling of the 2025 tungsten concentrate price would add
about USD 1,917 per tonne of Alloy C-276. Each USD 1,000 per tonne on LME nickel adds
about USD 10 per tonne of alloy for every percentage point of nickel, which the monthly
surcharge passes on (ATI, 2026a; Haynes International, 2023b; Product Strategy 2035 Team,
2026a; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026d). The severe aerospace case cuts aerospace output by
20 percent in 2026 and 2027 and recovers 70 percent of the lost level. Its 2035 aerospace

index is 114 (96 to 133), the superalloy family index falls from 129 to 117, and aviation cobalt
falls from 15.4 to 13.0 kt (Boeing, 2026a; Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).

A2.6 Scoring rules for the opportunity matrix
Table 17 scores opportunities for a producer that already melts and rolls high performance
alloys. Market, growth and supply security are computed from data by model/scoring.py. The
other five criteria are team judgement on the anchors of Table A4. The weighted score is the
sum of each score times its weight. Rows without measured volume (E) are capped at Option,
priority 1 starts at 3.75, priority 2 at 3.00 and Monitor at 2.75 (Product Strategy 2035 Team,
2026a).
Market uses the pool specific to the end use. Rows without a cobalt pool use the family tonnes
times an end use weight and EU unit values, capped at 4 because the weight is unmeasured.
Engine bar thus uses 193 kt times the aerospace weight of 0.575, about EUR 4.0 bn, an upper
bound for wrought bar because cast parts share the weight. Growth uses the most specific
driver: the engine delivery path for engine bar (3.7 percent a year), gas turbine output for
remelt stock (3.6), the SEMI path for fab gas strip (4.2) and the aftermarket for repair wire
(3.1).
Supply security takes the weakest input of the lead grades. A single source rule applies where
one country mines at least 90 percent of world output with no control in force. Such an
element scores 3 below 10 percent of the alloy and 2 above it. Alloy 718, with 4.75 to 5.50
percent niobium from Brazil (93 percent of world output) and at most 1 percent cobalt, scores
3. Cobalt bearing wrought grades such as Waspaloy, at about 13 percent cobalt under the
Congo quota, score 2 (Special Metals Corporation, 2007; U.S. Geological Survey, 2026h;
Wikipedia contributors, 2026).

The combined vacuum melt programme treats engine bar and turbine remelt stock as one melt
shop, with shares of 0.657 and 0.343. It uses the combined pool, the share weighted growth,
the weakest supply input and the share weighted judgement scores (Product Strategy 2035
Team, 2026a). The judgement values of the fab gas, NO96 and repair wire rows are first pass
team judgement.

A3     Data sources, product codes and reproducibility
The team collected structured data with the project's fetch script. The script saves raw files,
creates clean tables, and records each query's date and URL in a source register with more than
1,000 entries.
The UN Comtrade data cover HS 7502, 7504, 7505, 7506, 7507, 8105, 8109, 7219, 7220,
7223, 7409, 7606, 8311, and 8411 for ten reporting countries from 2019 to 2024. The HS 6
digit alloy codes 750512, 750522, 750620 and 750712 separate nickel alloys from pure nickel
for 2023 and 2024 and set the traded floors in A2.4 (Product Strategy 2035 Team, 2026a).
Eurostat PRODCOM covers fifteen nickel, cobalt, stainless, copper, and aluminium product
codes for the EU and five producer countries. Other sources include USGS Mineral
Commodity Summaries 2026, the World Bank Pink Sheet, OECD STAN and ICIO, UNIDO
INDSTAT for Germany, and filings from 61 companies.
Table 10 in chapter 6 estimates market values by multiplying EU apparent consumption by the
lower and higher of the 2024 unit values for exports and imports outside the EU. The team
used these figures because Eurostat production values fall below the value of the nickel
contained in the products.
For flat products, 57.8 kt × EUR 31.0 per kg gives EUR 1.79 bn, and 57.8 kt × EUR 47.8 per
kg gives EUR 2.76 bn. For tubes, 9.2 kt × EUR 26.1 per kg gives EUR 0.24 bn, and 9.2 kt ×
EUR 41.1 per kg gives EUR 0.38 bn. The team did not value bars because the production
figure is confidential (Eurostat, 2025, 2026c).
The team calculated unit values by dividing trade value by trade quantity in PRODCOM DS-
059358. The resulting range reflects the mix of grades traded across the EU border. It does not
represent a quoted list price.

A4     Limitations and further research
The study has three main limitations. First, official data stop at the mill product stage. HS 6
digit trade data separate nickel alloys from pure nickel, but PRODCOM production data do
not, and no source gives tonnes by alloy grade. A survey of producers or Comext data at the
eight digit level could sharpen the estimates.
                                                                                              55
Second, tier 1 uses published activity drivers, but its alloy intensity, use shares, and product
family weights are team assumptions. Nickel alloy tonnes by industry are not published, and
the backtest periods are short. The aerospace haircut rests on three points of one vintage, and
the gas turbine driver rests on Siemens Energy alone, because GE Vernova and Mitsubishi
Power data were not available. The team should rerun the model when the IEA World Energy
Outlook 2026, the next Airbus and Boeing outlooks, and new SEMI forecasts become
available.
Third, the trade analysis covers ten large reporting countries. A global analysis of mirror data
that includes Russia, Sweden, and Austria would give a fuller picture of trade routes.
